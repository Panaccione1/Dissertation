\chapter{Introduction}

Nuclear power occupies a singular position among low-carbon energy sources:
it is the only technology that delivers firm, dispatchable, high-density
electricity without direct greenhouse-gas emissions at a scale already
proven across decades of operation. Yet the environments that make nuclear
power possible, intense neutron and gamma fields, high temperatures, and
chemically aggressive primary coolants (e.g., liquid sodium, molten
fluoride salts, and high-pressure water), are precisely the environments
that destroy
the instruments needed to monitor and control it. Reliable radiation
monitoring is not incidental to safe reactor operation; it is the primary
mechanism by which operators verify that every defense-in-depth barrier
to fission-product release, fuel cladding, reactor pressure boundary,
and containment, remains intact. As the existing fleet ages and a generation of
advanced and small modular reactors (SMRs) moves from concept to
construction,
the instrumentation technologies that have served the industry for half a
century are confronting hard limits in both technical performance and
economic viability. This dissertation investigates two complementary
approaches to resolving that tension: the development of perovskite
semiconductors as radiation-responsive detector materials, and the
implementation of radiation-tolerant digital monitoring systems built on
commercially available field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs). Taken
together, these efforts span the full stack from detector material to
deployable system and aim to demonstrate that next-generation nuclear
instrumentation can be both technically capable and represent a meaningful
improvement in economic viability over current approaches.

\section{Nuclear Power and the Global Decarbonization Context}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The scale of the decarbonization challenge facing the global electricity
system makes any realistic pathway to net-zero heavily dependent on nuclear
energy. At the COP28 climate summit in December 2023, twenty-two nations
signed a declaration to triple global nuclear generating capacity from
approximately 375\,GWe to more than 1,125\,GWe by 2050; that coalition
has since grown to thirty-three countries~\cite{DOE_COP28_2023}. %% TODO: verify bib entry DOE_COP28_2023
The
International Energy Agency's net-zero pathway requires nuclear capacity to
reach approximately 1,079\,GWe by 2050, demanding that annual
investment in the sector roughly double from its current level of
\$65--75 billion to \$120--150 billion per year by 2030~\cite{IEA_Nuclear_2024}. %% TODO: verify bib entry IEA_Nuclear_2024
The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has revised its projections
upward for five consecutive years, with its 2025 high-case scenario projecting
992\,GWe by 2050~\cite{IAEA_Projections_2025}. %% TODO: verify bib entry IAEA_Projections_2025

The existing global fleet comprises approximately 408 operating reactors
in thirty-one countries, with a combined net capacity that reached a record
369\,GWe in 2024~\cite{WNISR_2025}. Nuclear electricity generation also
set an all-time high of 2,667\,TWh in 2024, narrowly surpassing the 2006
record, though nuclear's share of global electricity has fallen to
approximately 9\% as variable renewables have expanded at a far faster
pace~\cite{WNISR_2025}. The fleet's average age of 32.4 years, with 141
units exceeding 41 years of operation, makes life extension, instrumentation
modernization, and new construction simultaneously urgent priorities.

Development of advanced reactors is more active and better funded than
at any point in the last several decades. In the United
States, TerraPower's 345\,MW(e) Natrium sodium-cooled fast reactor received
its NRC construction permit in March 2026, the first commercial non-light-water
reactor approval in over forty years~\cite{PowerMag_Natrium_2026}. Kairos
Power's Hermes fluoride-salt-cooled test reactor began nuclear
safety-related construction in 2025~\cite{Kairos_Hermes_2025}, and
X-energy's Xe-100 pebble-bed high-temperature gas-cooled reactor is under
development for an industrial host site with over \$1.2 billion in DOE
cost-share~\cite{Xenergy_ARDP}. Among light-water SMRs, GE-Hitachi's
BWRX-300 is under construction at Ontario's Darlington site and has
received a \$400 million DOE award for deployment at TVA's Clinch River
site~\cite{BWRX300_Wiki}. Each of these programs introduces novel
coolant chemistries, fuel geometries, and multi-module control architectures
that existing monitoring technologies were never designed to address.

\section{The Regulatory Foundation for Radiation Monitoring}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Continuous radiation monitoring across neutron flux, gamma dose, primary
coolant activity, and effluent pathways is mandated by the regulatory
foundations of nuclear operation, not merely encouraged by best practice.
The NRC's General Design Criteria (GDC), codified in 10~CFR~50 Appendix~A,
establish this requirement at the highest level of the licensing framework.
GDC~13 requires instrumentation capable of monitoring all variables that
affect the reactor core, coolant pressure boundary, and containment integrity;
GDC~64 mandates monitoring of the containment atmosphere, recirculation
spaces, and effluent pathways for radioactivity; and GDC~19 requires that
control room operators maintain safe-shutdown capability with adequate
instrumentation even under accident conditions~\cite{NRC_GDC_2023}. The IAEA
mirrors these requirements through the Safety Requirements for Nuclear
Power Plants (SSR-2/1) and provides detailed I\&C system
guidance in SSG-39~\cite{IAEA_SSR21, IAEA_SSG39}.

The technical scope of these monitoring requirements is exceptionally broad. Neutron
flux measurement alone spans approximately twelve decades, from a few counts
per second during refueling to approximately $10^{15}$\,n\,cm$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$
at full power, requiring three overlapping detector subsystems. Source-range
monitors based on proportional counters or fission chambers operate in
pulse-counting mode across the lowest six to seven decades during startup and
shutdown. Intermediate-range monitors employ compensated ionization chambers
in Campbell mode across approximately eight to ten decades. Power-range
monitors using uncompensated boron-lined ionization chambers cover the upper
two decades and are calibrated against secondary-side heat balance. Each of
these subsystems must meet independent safety-channel redundancy requirements,
typically two to four independent channels per range~\cite{NucPower_Excore}.

Beyond neutron flux, plants deploy area gamma monitors throughout containment,
auxiliary buildings, fuel handling areas, and spent fuel pools to enforce
occupational dose limits under 10~CFR~20. On-line gamma spectroscopy of the
primary coolant monitors fission-product signatures that indicate
fuel-cladding breaches. Containment atmosphere monitors classified as
Category~1 under Regulatory Guide 1.97, Revision 5, must survive combined
temperature, pressure, radiation, and chemical-spray environments for the
full duration of a design-basis accident~\cite{NRC_RG197_2018}. Stack and
liquid effluent monitors verify compliance with 10~CFR~50 Appendix~I dose
objectives, while the Radiological Environmental Monitoring Program tracks
off-site air, water, and vegetation samples under RG~4.1.

The Fukushima Daiichi accident of March 2011 provided a severe demonstration
of the consequences when monitoring fails. The tsunami rendered 23 of 24
on-site radiation monitoring stations nonfunctional. The subsequent loss of
all AC power and battery depletion eliminated reactor vessel pressure,
temperature, and water-level indication, forcing operators to manage three
simultaneous core-melt progressions with essentially no real-time process
data~\cite{NCBI_Fukushima_Lessons, NAS_Fukushima_2016}. Spent fuel pool
instrumentation was unavailable for nearly two weeks. The NRC's
post-Fukushima response, including Order EA-12-051 requiring backup-powered
spent fuel pool level instrumentation, the FLEX diverse-coping strategy
described in NEI~12-06, and the addition of Type~F severe-accident management
variables to RG~1.97 Rev.~5, underscored that reliable monitoring is not a
compliance formality but the operational foundation of accident management.

\section{Limitations of Current Radiation Detector Technologies}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The detector materials deployed across the nuclear industry each carry
critical limitations that constrain their use in existing plants and threaten
to preclude their adoption in advanced reactor environments. High-purity
germanium (HPGe) remains the gold standard for gamma spectroscopy, achieving
better than 0.2\% full-width at half-maximum energy resolution at 662\,keV.
However, its narrow bandgap of 0.67\,eV mandates cryogenic operation below
120\,K. Liquid-nitrogen dewars are impractical for unattended or remote
monitoring; mechanical coolers add substantial cost, complexity, and
microphonic noise that degrades resolution at low energies. HPGe is therefore
confined almost exclusively to laboratory and assay applications rather than
continuous in-plant monitoring~\cite{Springer_HPGe_Cooling}.

Silicon detectors dominate charged-particle detection and, in research
settings, are used for fast-neutron spectrometry via proton-recoil
interactions~\cite{Nature_SiDamage}, but suffer irreversible displacement
damage above approximately $10^{14}$--$10^{15}$\,n$_\mathrm{eq}$\,cm$^{-2}$
fluence, with charge-collection efficiency falling toward 50\%, a threshold
easily surpassed in the lifetime of a power-reactor
instrument~\cite{Nature_SiDamage}. Silicon's low atomic number ($Z = 14$)
also provides poor photoelectric stopping power for gamma-ray energies above
a few hundred keV, making it poorly suited to the mixed neutron-gamma fields
present throughout a reactor plant.

Scintillators constitute the most widely deployed class of gamma-detection
instruments and are valued for their ability to produce measurable light from
a wide range of ionizing radiations. Sodium iodide thallium-doped
[NaI(Tl)] crystals, the workhorse material, achieve approximately 6--7\%
energy resolution at 662\,keV but are highly hygroscopic, mechanically
fragile, and must be coupled to photomultiplier tubes that are sensitive to
magnetic fields, fragile under shock and vibration, and require high-voltage
supplies of 800--1200\,V~\cite{Luxium_NaI}. Advanced scintillators such as
LaBr$_3$(Ce) achieve approximately 3\% resolution but contain intrinsic
radioactivity from $^{138}$La that creates a flat background continuum
complicating low-level measurements. Organic scintillators, including
plastic scintillators and stilbene crystals, are well established for fast
neutron detection through proton-recoil interactions, but their low effective
atomic number renders them essentially transparent to gamma rays, requiring
either active discrimination electronics or companion gamma-shielding that
adds mass, cost, and complexity.

Cadmium zinc telluride (CZT) represents the most mature room-temperature
semiconductor alternative, offering energy resolutions approaching 0.7\%
at 662\,keV in pixelated geometries with full single-carrier-transport
correction. However, CZT crystal growth requires extremely precise control
of stoichiometry and vapor-phase conditions during directional
solidification: high cadmium vapor pressure promotes vacancy defects,
tellurium inclusions limit boule diameter, and critical shear stresses
of only 0.3\,MPa make the material mechanically fragile during
processing~\cite{CZT_Wikipedia}. Production costs remain high, supply is
constrained, and the situation deteriorated significantly in 2025 when
the People's Republic of China imposed export controls on germanium,
gallium, and related critical materials including cadmium telluride compounds.

For thermal neutron detection, the $^3$He tube has been the standard
technology since the 1950s by virtue of its high neutron absorption
cross-section (5330\,barns at 0.025\,eV), excellent gamma discrimination,
and compatibility with conventional pulse-counting electronics. The
supply of $^3$He, produced exclusively as a decay product of tritium from
the nuclear-weapons stockpile, was recognized as critically constrained
following the post-September~11 deployment of approximately 1,300 radiation
portal monitors for border-security applications. Annual demand reached
roughly 70,000~L against a production rate of only approximately
8,000~L\,yr$^{-1}$, driving prices from approximately \$100\,L$^{-1}$ to
over \$2,000\,L$^{-1}$ and prompting DOE to begin rationing allocation in
2009~\cite{PhysicsToday_He3}. The episode exposed the fragility of depending
on a single non-renewable detection medium and has accelerated development
of alternative neutron detectors using $^{10}$B-lined tubes and
$^6$LiF:ZnS scintillator screens.

Halide perovskites have emerged as a compelling alternative class of
radiation-responsive materials. Their high effective atomic number, CsPbBr$_3$
has $Z_\mathrm{eff} = 65.9$, compared to 50.2 for CZT, provides excellent
photoelectric stopping power for gamma radiation~\cite{Springer_CsPbBr3}.
Single-crystal MAPbBr$_3$ and CsPbBr$_3$ devices, operating at room
temperature, have demonstrated
mobility-lifetime products of up to $8 \times 10^{-3}$\,cm$^2$\,V$^{-1}$
and energy resolutions approaching 1.4\% at 662\,keV~\cite{He_NatPhoton_2021},
approaching CZT performance without cryogenic cooling or Bridgman growth.
A distinctive self-healing mechanism, in which the soft perovskite lattice
allows radiation-displaced ions to migrate back toward equilibrium positions
at room temperature, has been confirmed under proton irradiation to
$10^{13}$\,p\,cm$^{-2}$ and extended gamma exposures~\cite{RG_SelfHealing}.
The methylhydrazinium lead chloride (MHyPbCl$_3$) composition investigated
in this work introduces a high-hydrogen-density 3D perovskite framework
with a hydrogen density of $3.79 \times 10^{22}$\,cm$^{-3}$ comparable to
organic scintillators, enabling direct fast-neutron conversion through proton
recoil without requiring a separate moderator or conversion
layer~\cite{Panaccione_NIM_2024}.

Significant challenges remain. Ion migration under applied electric fields
and elevated humidity drives hysteresis in current-voltage characteristics
and accelerates device failure at the biases required for thick-crystal
direct-conversion operation. Long-term stability data at power-reactor
fluence levels ($10^{14}$--$10^{15}$\,n\,cm$^{-2}$) remain absent from the
literature. Lead toxicity presents regulatory and environmental considerations
for deployed instruments. These challenges motivate the stabilization
strategies, fluorinated surface passivation, hexagonal boron nitride
composites, and vacuum operation, investigated in the present work.

\section{The Economics of Nuclear Instrumentation}
\label{sec:intro_economics}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Cost overruns in nuclear construction have become the defining obstacle to
expansion in liberalized electricity markets. The two AP1000 units completed
at Plant Vogtle in Georgia cost approximately \$35 billion against an original
estimate of \$14 billion~\cite{AJC_Vogtle}. France's Flamanville~3 EPR
reached \texteuro{}23.7 billion, seven times its original budget, after
seventeen years of construction. The UK's Hinkley Point C has escalated to
an estimated £35 billion in 2015 prices against an initial projection of
approximately £18 billion~\cite{CounterPunch_HPC}. New nuclear levelized
cost of electricity in the United States ranges from approximately
\$110\,MWh$^{-1}$ (EIA) to \$180\,MWh$^{-1}$ (Lazard), compared to
approximately \$55\,MWh$^{-1}$ for utility-scale solar and
\$40\,MWh$^{-1}$ for onshore wind. The existing U.S. fleet operates far
more competitively, NEI reports a 2023 average total generating cost of
\$31.76\,MWh$^{-1}$, the lowest on record~\cite{NEI_Costs_2025}, but
twelve commercial reactors have closed since 2013 due to competitive
pressure, and emergency support programs including a \$6 billion Civil
Nuclear Credit allocation have been required to prevent further
premature retirements~\cite{NBC_CivilNuclear}.

Instrumentation and control systems, while representing an estimated 5--10\%
of total plant capital cost, exert a disproportionate influence on both
construction timelines and long-term operating expenses. A typical large
light-water reactor contains approximately 10,000 sensors, 5,000~km of
I\&C cabling, and hundreds of electronic cabinets. Nuclear-grade quality
assurance requirements under 10~CFR~50 Appendix~B impose a cost premium that
pervades the entire supply chain: components manufactured to identical
functional specifications have been documented at price ratios of 4:1 or
greater between nuclear-qualified and commercial-grade procurement, driven
by QA documentation requirements, environmental qualification testing, and
the severe scarcity of nuclear-qualified suppliers~\cite{ANS_ICModernization_2025}.
Dominion Energy's comprehensive digital I\&C modernization across four units
at Surry and North Anna is budgeted at \$5.45 billion as part of subsequent
license renewal~\cite{PowerEng_DigitalAge}. The DOE Light Water Reactor
Sustainability (LWRS) Program has explicitly cautioned that
``wide-scale modernization of legacy I\&C systems could prove to be
cost-prohibitive unless implemented to enable significant business innovation.''

The cost of radiation-hardened digital electronics amplifies this problem
at the component level. Purpose-built radiation-hardened FPGAs, the
Microchip RTG4, AMD/Xilinx XQRKU060, and their equivalents, carry price
tags of tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars per device,
a premium of 10$\times$ to 100$\times$ over commercial equivalents that
cost \$10--500~\cite{BISResearch_RadHardFPGA}. The radiation-hardened
FPGA market, valued at \$465.5 million in 2024, serves primarily space
and defense applications where unit volumes are small and alternatives
do not exist. These devices trail commercial FPGAs by two to three process
generations: the Microchip RTG4 uses a 65~nm flash-based process while
commercial devices operate at 3--5~nm, a gap representing multiple orders
of magnitude in logic density, performance, and power
efficiency~\cite{Microchip_RTG4, AMD_XQRKU060}. Long lead times of 6--18
months, ITAR export restrictions, and the absence of advanced intellectual
property cores compound the challenge. For SMRs targeting a levelized cost
of electricity of \$60--90\,MWh$^{-1}$, this cost structure is untenable:
I\&C requirements do not scale down proportionally with reactor output,
making instrumentation a higher fraction of total cost in smaller plants.
The DOE's GEMINA program has set an aspirational target of reducing
advanced reactor operations and maintenance cost from \$23\,MWh$^{-1}$
to \$2\,MWh$^{-1}$ through autonomous operations and advanced diagnostics,
a goal that cannot be achieved without fundamentally cheaper and more
capable sensing and processing hardware~\cite{DOE_GEMINA}.

The alternative to purpose-built rad-hard electronics is software-defined
radiation tolerance using commercial off-the-shelf FPGAs with designer-inserted
mitigation. Triple modular redundancy (TMR) triplicates critical logic with
majority voting to correct single-event upsets in real time; configuration
memory scrubbing periodically corrects bit-flip errors in SRAM-based
FPGAs before they propagate; and partial dynamic reconfiguration enables
real-time repair of corrupted fabric regions. CERN's two-year study of
Kintex-7 FPGAs under mixed-field radiation found no permanent operational
failures to 300\,krad total ionizing dose with TMR and
scrubbing~\cite{CERN_FPGA_Rad}. Montana State University's RadSat program
demonstrated COTS FPGA-based radiation tolerance at ``a cost 100$\times$
lower than existing radiation-hardened computers.'' A TMR-with-spares
architecture for nuclear reactor protection system logic has been
validated under cyclotron irradiation~\cite{SciDirect_TMR_Nuclear}. These
results suggest that COTS FPGAs with mature mitigation strategies can meet
nuclear monitoring requirements at a fraction of the cost of purpose-built
rad-hard devices, provided the mitigation architecture is co-designed with
the application from the outset.

\section{Research Objectives and Dissertation Organization}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The research presented in this dissertation is organized around four
complementary research thrusts aligned with the materials-to-systems
hierarchy described above.

The first thrust characterizes the radiation response of
photovoltaic-architecture halide perovskite solar cells operating as
current-mode radiation monitors.  Cs$_{0.1}$FA$_{0.9}$PbI$_3$ devices
are evaluated under X-ray, $^{137}$Cs gamma, and $^{60}$Co gamma
irradiation to establish the current-mode detection mechanism,
dose-rate scaling, and long-term degradation behavior.

The second thrust develops and demonstrates perovskite semiconductor
radiation detectors for direct-conversion operation under applied bias.
This encompasses X-ray detection with MAPbI$_3$-hBN composite and
two-dimensional perovskite devices, gamma spectroscopy with bromide
perovskite single crystals, and fast-neutron detection using the
hydrogen-rich MHyPbCl$_3$ composition.  Ion migration under applied
electric fields is identified and addressed as the central
performance-limiting mechanism across all detector systems.

The third thrust investigates thermal radiation sensing as an
alternative, non-electronic monitoring pathway for nuclear environments.
Analytical thermal models are developed and experimentally validated for
an Optical Fiber-Based Gamma Thermometer (OFBGT), and a proposed Optical
Fiber-Based Neutron Dosimeter (OFBND) is evaluated through simulation.

The fourth thrust implements and validates radiation-tolerant FPGA-based
digital instrumentation for nuclear monitoring.  Two complementary
benchmarks, a Flip-Flop Shift Register (FFSR) exercising
reconfiguration and readback paths, and a fixed-point thermal control
loop stressing arithmetic, control, and state evolution, are tested
under fast neutron irradiation to characterize configuration memory
upsets, latch-up susceptibility, and total ionizing dose effects in
SRAM-based FPGAs.

The remainder of this dissertation is organized as follows. Chapter~2
provides the perovskite-focused background necessary for the materials and
device investigations, encompassing perovskite crystal structure, radiation
interaction physics, ion migration mechanisms, and the current state of
perovskite-based radiation detectors. Chapter~3 provides the digital
electronics background, covering radiation effects on semiconductor devices,
single-event effects in SRAM-based FPGAs, and the taxonomy of fault-tolerant
design approaches including triple modular redundancy and configuration
scrubbing.  Chapter~4 presents the characterization of
Cs$_{0.1}$FA$_{0.9}$PbI$_3$ perovskite solar cells as current-mode
radiation monitors under extended gamma irradiation.  Chapter~5
consolidates the direct-conversion perovskite detector investigations,
covering X-ray detection with MAPbI$_3$-hBN and two-dimensional
perovskite devices, gamma spectroscopy with FAPbBr$_3$ and CsPbBr$_3$
single crystals, and fast-neutron detection with MHyPbCl$_3$.  Chapter~6
describes the thermal radiation sensing concepts, including the OFBGT
simulation, experimental validation, and the proposed OFBND design.
Chapter~7 presents the FFSR digital electronics verification structure
and its experimental results under fast neutron irradiation at the Ohio
State University Research Reactor.  Chapter~8 extends the FPGA
irradiation campaign with a fixed-point thermal control benchmark that
couples closed-loop feedback control, emulated plant dynamics, and
dual-modular redundancy.  Chapter~9 synthesizes the principal findings
across all four research thrusts and identifies directions for future
work.
