\chapter{Perovskite Semiconductor Radiation Detectors}
\label{ch:perovskite_detectors}

The preceding chapter established that photovoltaic-architecture
perovskite thin films can generate measurable current-mode signals
in response to X-ray, gamma, and neutron irradiation, exploiting
the device's built-in electric field to sweep radiation-generated
carriers to the contacts without any externally applied bias.  The
present chapter pursues a fundamentally different detection
philosophy: deploying halide perovskites as direct-conversion
semiconductor radiation detectors in which a large externally
applied reverse bias sweeps charge carriers generated by individual
radiation interaction events across the device and into a
charge-sensitive readout chain.  This mode of operation, familiar
from conventional compound semiconductor detectors such as
CdZnTe (CZT) and Cd\-Te, offers the possibility of pulse-by-pulse
energy spectroscopy rather than the integrated current monitoring
that characterises the photovoltaic geometry of
Chapter~\ref{ch:CsFAPbI}.

Halide perovskites present a compelling case for direct-conversion
detector development.  Their heavy-metal cation sublattice,
dominated by lead in the systems studied here, provides a high
effective atomic number, giving large photoelectric cross sections
at gamma and X-ray energies that are difficult or impossible to
achieve with silicon or organic semiconductors.  Some
methylhydrazinium-based compositions additionally incorporate a
high density of hydrogen atoms, enabling elastic proton-recoil
reactions with fast neutrons that allow direct-conversion neutron
detection without requiring a separate conversion layer.
Solution-phase crystal growth methods have produced
centimetre-scale single crystals with carrier $\mu\tau$ products
approaching or exceeding those of polycrystalline CZT, a property
critical for efficient charge collection across the
millimetre-scale thicknesses required to stop MeV-energy photons
and neutrons.

This chapter is organised by device, with each section covering a
single detector system from fabrication through characterisation to
radiation detection results.  Six devices are presented: the
MAPbI$_3$-hBN X-ray detector
(Section~\ref{sec:mapbi_hbn_xray}), the 2D perovskite
(PEA$_2$MA$_2$Pb$_3$I$_{10}$) X-ray detector with 5F-PEAI surface
treatment (Section~\ref{sec:2d_perovskite_xray}), the
CsPbBr$_3$ gamma-ray detector
(Section~\ref{sec:cspbbr_gamma}), the FAPbBr$_3$ gamma-ray
detector (Section~\ref{sec:fapbbr_gamma}), the MAPbBr$_3$ gamma
detector (Section~\ref{sec:mapbbr_issues}), and the MHyPbCl$_3$
fast neutron detector
(Section~\ref{sec:neutron_detector}).  A cross-device discussion
of ion migration, the central performance-limiting mechanism
observed across all systems, is presented in
Section~\ref{sec:ion_migration_detector}, followed by a summary
in Section~\ref{sec:ch5_summary}.

\section{Direct-Conversion Detector Principles}
\label{sec:detector_principles}
%% ============================================================

A direct-conversion radiation detector converts the energy
deposited by an ionising particle or photon directly into an
electrical signal, without an intermediate scintillation step.
When a gamma ray, X-ray, or charged particle deposits energy $E$
in a semiconductor detector of bandgap $E_g$, approximately
$E/W$ electron-hole pairs are created, where
$W \approx 2.8\,E_g$ is the mean ionisation energy per
pair~\cite{knoll2010}.  The carriers are swept by an applied
electric field to opposite contacts, where they induce a charge
pulse on the readout electronics.  For spectroscopic operation,
this pulse must be faithfully proportional to the deposited
energy, a condition that requires complete charge collection
and minimal trapping of carriers en route to the contacts.

The probability that a carrier generated at depth $x$ in the
crystal (of thickness $d$) reaches the collecting contact is
described by the Hecht equation,
\begin{equation}
  \eta = \frac{\mu\tau E}{d}
    \left[1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{\mu\tau E}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:hecht}
\end{equation}
where $E = V/d$ is the applied electric field, $V$ is the applied
voltage, and $\mu\tau$ is the mobility-lifetime product of the
dominant carrier species~\cite{Hofstadter1949}.
Equation~(\ref{eq:hecht}) shows that achieving high collection
efficiency $\eta \to 1$ requires either a large $\mu\tau$ product
or a large applied field, or both.  For a 1-mm-thick crystal with
$\mu\tau = 10^{-3}$~cm$^2$\,V$^{-1}$, complete collection
requires fields of order 10~V\,$\mu$m$^{-1}$, i.e.\ applied
voltages of order 1~kV.  This requirement for large applied
voltages is in direct tension with the ion-migration instability
described in Section~\ref{sec:ion_migration_detector}.

Two additional material requirements govern detector performance.
First, the dark resistivity $\rho$ must be sufficiently high that
the leakage current $I_{\mathrm{dark}} = V\!A/(\rho d)$ produces an
electronic noise contribution smaller than the signal charge from
the smallest radiation event of interest.  For gamma spectroscopy
with CZT-like performance,
$\rho > 10^{9}$~$\Omega$\,cm is typically
required~\cite{Luke1995}.  The 3D halide perovskites studied in
this work achieve resistivities in the range
$10^{9}$--$10^{12}$~$\Omega$\,cm in their highest-quality
single-crystal forms, competitive with or exceeding that of
polycrystalline CZT.  Second, the crystal must be thick enough to
stop a significant fraction of the incident radiation.  The mean
free path of a 662-keV gamma ray (the primary emission of
$^{137}$Cs) in CsPbBr$_3$ is approximately 7~mm, meaning that
crystals of order 5--10~mm are required for reasonable detection
efficiency.

For fast neutron detection, the governing interaction is elastic
proton recoil: a fast neutron of kinetic energy $E_n$ collides
with a hydrogen nucleus, transferring up to $E_n$ of kinetic
energy to the recoil proton in a head-on collision.  The recoil
proton then deposits its energy densely in the crystal volume.
The detection efficiency of a hydrogenous material is therefore
proportional to its hydrogen atom density $n_H$.
Table~\ref{tab:h_density} compares $n_H$ for the key materials
considered in this work.

\section{MAPbI$_3$-hBN X-Ray Detector}
\label{sec:mapbi_hbn_xray}
%% ============================================================

The MAPbI$_3$-hBN device was the primary polycrystalline X-ray
detector fabricated and characterised in this work.  Its chief
contribution is establishing the role of atmospheric humidity in
limiting perovskite detector performance under applied bias, and
demonstrating that vacuum operation partially restores intrinsic
carrier transport.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Material and Device Fabrication}
\label{sec:mapbi_fabrication}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

MAPbI$_3$ (methylammonium lead triiodide) doped with hexagonal
boron nitride (hBN) was used as the X-ray absorber medium.  The
hBN inclusions were incorporated to improve grain boundary
passivation and reduce the density of deep-level trap states that
would otherwise act as recombination centres for
radiation-generated carriers.  The contact architecture was
asymmetric, with the hBN/MAPbI$_3$ layer sandwiched between
contacts of differing work function.  This asymmetry creates a
built-in potential that enables a photovoltaic-like X-ray response
even at zero applied bias, as demonstrated below.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/5_mapbi_hbn_device_photo}
  \caption{MAPbI$_3$-hBN X-ray detector mounted for electrical testing.}
  \label{fig:mapbi_device_photo}
\end{figure}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Electrical Characterisation}
\label{sec:mapbi_electrical}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Current-voltage (IV) characteristics were measured using a
Keithley 2612A SourceMeter, with sweep protocols covering $\pm$2~V
for contact resistance extraction up to high-voltage unipolar
sweeps (0 to $-200$~V) for leakage characterisation under detector
operating conditions.  All sweeps were performed in a light-tight
enclosure to prevent photogenerated carrier contributions.  IV
hysteresis measurements at 20~mV\,s$^{-1}$ served as a diagnostic
for ion-migration-driven charge redistribution; the MAPbI$_3$-hBN
device exhibited a measurable sweep-versus-hold difference in the
low-voltage regime, indicative of a slow ionic relaxation
polarisation effect consistent with field-driven amplification of
the ion-migration rate.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{X-Ray Sensitivity Measurements}
\label{sec:mapbi_sensitivity}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

X-ray characterisation was performed using a benchtop X-ray tube
operated at 30--50~kVp, collimated to a conical beam with an
$\sim$2~cm exit aperture.  The photocurrent was measured as a
function of applied bias with the X-ray beam switched on and off
periodically to distinguish the radiation-induced signal from the
dark-current baseline.  X-ray sensitivity $S$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
  S = \frac{\Delta I}{A \cdot \dot{D}},
  \label{eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta I$ is the X-ray-induced change in current, $A$ is
the active contact area, and $\dot{D}$ is the calibrated dose
rate.  The dose rate was calibrated using a RaySafe 452 dosimeter;
however, the conical beam geometry caused dose-rate variation
across the dosimeter face, leading to underestimation of the dose
rate incident on the small perovskite contact area.  NanoDot
optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dosimeters were identified
as a more appropriate calibration tool for subsequent experiments.

Sensitivity increased monotonically with applied bias:
$0.107~\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ at 5~V,
$0.245~\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ at 10~V (confirmed by repeat
measurement at $0.252~\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$), and
$0.411~\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ at 20~V.  The sub-linear
voltage dependence, a factor of $\sim$3.8 sensitivity gain for
a factor-of-four voltage increase, indicates the device had not
yet entered the charge-collection saturation regime at 20~V.

A bi-polar X-ray response was observed at zero applied bias in
some device configurations: a spontaneous photovoltaic-like
current transient was generated by X-ray exposure without any
external voltage.  This is attributed to the asymmetric contact
architecture creating a built-in potential difference between the
hBN/MAPbI$_3$ and Au contact interfaces, which sweeps
radiation-generated carriers toward the respective contacts even
in the absence of applied bias.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Vacuum Testing and Humidity Effects}
\label{sec:mapbi_vacuum}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

To directly evaluate the role of atmospheric humidity, X-ray
sensitivity measurements were repeated under vacuum (base
pressure $< 10^{-3}$~Torr) and compared to measurements in
ambient laboratory air at matched bias voltages of 5, 10, and
20~V.  In every case, the X-ray-induced current was larger and
more stable under vacuum, and the dark-current baseline was lower.
These improvements are consistent with suppression of
moisture-accelerated ion migration: water molecules at grain
boundaries facilitate halide vacancy diffusion under the applied
field, creating shunting pathways and reducing the effective bulk
field.  Removing ambient moisture under vacuum disrupts this
pathway and partially restores intrinsic carrier transport.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/5_mapbi_hbn_xray_vacuum}
  \caption{X-ray current transients for the MAPbI$_3$-hBN device at 5, 10, and 20~V bias in ambient air and under vacuum.}
  \label{fig:mapbi_vacuum}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Alpha Spectroscopy Under Vacuum}
\label{sec:mapbi_alpha}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Alpha particles deposit their energy very densely within a few
micrometres of the crystal surface and generate a correspondingly
large number of electron-hole pairs, for a 5.49-MeV alpha from
Am-241 and $W \approx 2.8\,E_g \approx 8$~eV per pair in
MAPbI$_3$, approximately $6.8 \times 10^5$ carriers are generated
per event, roughly three orders of magnitude more than a single
662-keV gamma interaction.  Despite this large signal charge,
alpha spectroscopy had not been attempted with halide perovskite
detectors at the time of this work because vacuum operation,
which is required to position a source in close proximity without
air attenuation of the short-range alpha particles, was known
to increase the leakage current of perovskite devices, rendering
pulse-height analysis impractical.  The physical origin of this
vacuum-induced leakage increase is not well established; proposed
explanations include a reduction in surface resistance as adsorbed
surface species are removed under evacuation, and electrical
disturbances arising from outgassing of volatile components from
the perovskite film.

The MAPbI$_3$-hBN device was anomalous in this respect: under
vacuum (base pressure $< 10^{-3}$~Torr), the leakage current
decreased rather than increased, yielding a stable, low dark
current baseline over extended bias hold periods.  This behaviour
distinguished the device from other perovskite systems tested in
this programme and made alpha spectroscopy feasible for the first
time.  With the noise floor below the alpha signal level, a
resolvable Am-241 spectrum was obtained, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mapbi_am241}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{figures/5_mapbi_hbn_am241_vacuum}
  \caption{Am-241 spectrum and device stability data for the MAPbI$_3$-hBN detector under vacuum operation.}
  \label{fig:mapbi_am241}
\end{figure}

The broader significance of this result was subsequently
confirmed by independent groups.  Zhang et al.\ reported
high-resolution alpha particle spectroscopy with a CsPbBr$_3$
single-crystal detector, achieving an energy resolution of
6.9\% with commercial electronics and 1.1\% with a full-custom
readout ASIC designed to minimise parasitic capacitance and
electronic noise~\cite{Zhang2024alpha}.  That work achieved
stable low leakage in ambient conditions through crystal surface
thinning to remove the high-concentration CsPb$_2$Br$_5$ phase
combined with a Schottky electrode geometry, enabling a dark
current of 34.6~nA\,cm$^{-2}$ at 200~V without vacuum
enclosure.

Despite these improvements, the MAPbI$_3$-hBN device failed
irreversibly after approximately five hours of continuous
operation at 400~V applied bias.  Post-failure inspection showed
the device could no longer sustain any significant reverse bias,
consistent with the formation of a low-resistance shunting pathway
through the crystal bulk.  This failure mode is attributed to
catastrophic redistribution of ionic species under the large
field: as mobile iodide vacancies accumulate at the electrode
interfaces, the local electric field increases dramatically,
eventually driving decomposition of the perovskite lattice into
its constituent binary and ternary phases.  Vacuum operation
delays but does not eliminate this failure mode.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Charge Trapping and Sensitivity Degradation}
\label{sec:mapbi_trapping}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

A more gradual form of sensitivity degradation, distinct from
the acute failure at 400~V, was observed under continuous
X-ray exposure at moderate bias.  During prolonged irradiation at
$-50$~V, the X-ray-induced current decreased monotonically over
several minutes even though the incident X-ray flux was constant.
A comparable decay was recorded at $+50$~V.

The trapping phenomenon is attributed to progressive filling of
mid-gap trap states within the crystal.  Carriers trapped at
these sites are immobilised and cannot contribute to measured
current; additionally, the trapped charge builds an opposing
space-charge field that reduces the net electric field for
subsequently generated carriers.  Trapping occurs on a timescale
of seconds to minutes (governed by trap capture cross sections),
whereas ion migration proceeds on timescales of tens of minutes
to hours (governed by ionic diffusivity).  The distinction has
implications for mitigation: trap-state filling can be reversed
by briefly grounding the device to allow trapped carriers to
recombine or detrap thermally, whereas ionic redistribution
requires extended equilibration and may in extreme cases represent
permanent structural damage.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:mapbi_discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The absolute sensitivity values of the MAPbI$_3$-hBN device,
of order $10^{-1}$~$\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ at modest bias
voltages, are substantially below those of optimised 2D
perovskite thin-film detectors (Section~\ref{sec:2d_perovskite_xray}),
reflecting the thick absorber geometry rather than any intrinsic
material limitation.  This device establishes two critical
findings: first, that sensitivity follows the Hecht equation field
dependence, confirming charge collection, not absorption, is
the performance-limiting factor at these bias levels; and second,
that vacuum operation demonstrably suppresses the
moisture-accelerated degradation pathway, directly motivating the
5F-PEAI surface treatment strategy demonstrated in the next
section.


%% ============================================================
\section{2D Perovskite (PEA$_2$MA$_2$Pb$_3$I$_{10}$) X-Ray
         Detector with 5F-PEAI Surface Treatment}
\label{sec:2d_perovskite_xray}
%% ============================================================

The voltage-induced instability observed in the MAPbI$_3$-hBN
device is a general challenge for all perovskite direct-conversion
detectors requiring sustained reverse bias.  To address this in
polycrystalline thin-film detectors, Tsai et al.\ developed a
fluorinated surface treatment strategy~\cite{Tsai2022ionmig},
with the present author contributing to device fabrication and
X-ray characterisation.  This section presents that work in full
as it directly motivates the mitigation strategies discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:ion_migration_detector}.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Device Structure and Material Characterisation}
\label{sec:2d_device_char}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The 2D perovskite detector employed a p--i--n diode architecture.
The p-type contact was poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)
amine] (PTAA) and the n-type contact was a fullerene (60~nm) /
bathocuproine BCP (10~nm) bilayer, completed with a gold top
contact without additional encapsulation.  The intrinsic
perovskite layer targeted a PEA$_2$MA$_2$Pb$_3$I$_{10}$
composition (PEA = phenylethylammonium).  Cross-sectional SEM
showed a continuous, compact film approximately 9~$\mu$m thick,
with large crystal grains extending through most of the film
thickness and a more disordered, smaller-crystallite structure
near the surface.  Synchrotron GIWAXS confirmed high
crystallinity with strong discrete Bragg spots and weak
Debye--Scherrer rings from the surface-near disordered layer.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Humidity-Dependent Instability}
\label{sec:2d_humidity}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Devices were tested at controlled relative humidity (RH) of 0\%,
20\%, and 40\% in a sealed chamber.  At 0\% RH, devices exhibited
no measurable I--V hysteresis and remained stable to voltages
beyond $-15$~V.  As RH was raised to 40\%, hysteresis became
pronounced and breakdown voltage fell dramatically: devices failed
irreversibly between $-8$ and $-10$~V, versus more than twice
that at 0\% RH.  Dark current measurements at $-5$~V in 40\% RH
air showed device failure within two to three minutes.  The
combination of reverse bias and humidity was identified as the
critical driver of failure, moisture alone was insufficient to
cause instantaneous breakdown.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{5F-PEAI Surface Treatment and Results}
\label{sec:5fpeai_results}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

A fluorinated phenylethylammonium iodide (5F-PEAI) barrier layer
was applied to the perovskite surface, replacing all five hydrogen
atoms on the PEAI phenyl ring with fluorine.  This increased the
water contact angle from 66.1$^\circ$ to 76.0$^\circ$
($\approx$15\%), confirming enhanced hydrophobicity.  At the
optimal concentration of 4~mg\,mL$^{-1}$, 5F-PEAI penetrated
grain boundaries and stabilised the device to $-16$~V in 40\% RH,
compared to $-10$~V for the untreated device.  ToF-SIMS
fluorine mapping confirmed grain boundary penetration at the
higher concentration.

DFT calculations revealed the mechanism: the PEA surface has a
water penetration activation energy of only $\sim$5~meV, far
below room-temperature thermal energy, whereas the fluorinated
5F-PEA surface raises this barrier to $\sim$120~meV, with strong
electrostatic repulsion from electronegative fluorine atoms
preventing moisture ingress.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{X-Ray Detection Performance}
\label{sec:2d_xray_performance}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

EQE measurements showed that 5F-PEAI-coated devices reached 50\%
at $-10$~V reverse bias, compared to 30\% saturation for the
uncoated device.  X-ray sensitivity at 4~V bias reached an
average of $862.5~\mu$C\,(Gy$_{\mathrm{air}}$\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ (vs.\
$726.2~\mu$C\,(Gy$_{\mathrm{air}}$\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ for the reference),
with a maximum of $996.3~\mu$C\,(Gy$_{\mathrm{air}}$\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$,
among the highest reported for polycrystalline perovskite
detectors of comparable absorber thickness.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:2d_discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The sensitivity values of the 5F-PEAI-treated 2D detector
($\sim$$10^3$~$\mu$C\,(Gy$_{\mathrm{air}}$\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$) exceed
those of the MAPbI$_3$-hBN device ($\sim$$10^{-1}$~$\mu$C)
by three to four orders of magnitude, reflecting device geometry
differences rather than intrinsic material quality.  The 5F-PEAI
surface treatment demonstrates a clear pathway for stabilising
polycrystalline perovskite detectors against moisture-accelerated
breakdown.  Extending this passivation strategy to the 3D single
crystal systems used for gamma-ray and neutron detection is
identified as a high-priority direction for future work.

\section{CsPbBr$_3$ Gamma-Ray Detector}
\label{sec:cspbbr_gamma}
%% ============================================================

CsPbBr$_3$ was the first bromide perovskite single crystal
evaluated as a gamma-ray spectrometer in this work.  Its wide
bandgap ($\sim$2.3~eV), large effective atomic number, and high
reported resistivity made it a natural candidate for high-voltage
spectroscopy.  In practice, the device was significantly limited
by large, unstable leakage current at the operating bias required
for reasonable charge collection.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Crystal Preparation and Contact Fabrication}
\label{sec:cspbbr_fabrication}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

CsPbBr$_3$ single crystals were grown externally and received by
the research group.  Crystals judged free of macroscopic defects
were mechanically planarised using silicon carbide abrasive paper,
then sequentially polished with diamond polishing paste to a
10-nm surface finish.  A hexane bath rinse was performed between
each polishing stage to remove residual abrasive particles.  One
CsPbBr$_3$ crystal sustained internal cracking from an accidental
drop during handling; while it remained electrically functional,
the crack likely introduced additional carrier trapping sites and
reduced the geometric detection efficiency.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/5_cspbbr_polishing}
  \caption{CsPbBr$_3$ crystal during mechanical polishing preparation.}
  \label{fig:cspbbr_polishing}
\end{figure}

The Schottky contact architecture, common to CsPbBr$_3$,
MAPbBr$_3$, and FAPbBr$_3$, is shown schematically in
Figure~\ref{fig:bromide_contact_schematic}.  The final device
design, which produced the best spectroscopic results with the
FAPbBr$_3$ crystal, used gold as the pixelated contact and
Ti/Ni as a continuous back-face contact.  A 2$\times$2 array
of 1.25-mm-diameter gold pixel contacts surrounded by an
integrated guard ring was deposited through a drilled aluminium
shadow mask using a Denton sputter coater.  On the opposite
crystal face, a continuous Ti/Ni bilayer was deposited using
an AJA Orion RF/DC sputter system: Ti was deposited at 450~W
($\approx$1.15~\AA\,s$^{-1}$) followed immediately by a 10-nm
Ni passivating overlayer at 250~W
($\approx$2.1~\AA\,s$^{-1}$) across the full crystal face.
Pixel contacts were wired via Cu leads bonded with silver epoxy.

The necessity of the Ni overlayer was established empirically
during early contact deposition trials, in which Ti was deposited
without any capping layer.  Upon venting the deposition chamber
to atmosphere, the bare Ti films were observed to delaminate from
the crystal surface in a characteristic rolled pattern, visible
in Figure~\ref{fig:ti_delamination}.  The root cause is the
oxidation of Ti upon air exposure: as the surface of the Ti film
converts to TiO$_x$, the crystallographic lattice mismatch
between the oxide and the underlying metallic Ti generates a
large in-plane compressive stress.  This biaxial stress causes
the film to buckle and curl away from the perovskite surface,
producing the ``swiss-roll'' delamination morphology observed.
The problem was fully resolved by depositing the 10-nm Ni
capping layer immediately after Ti, before venting, which
prevents atmospheric oxygen from reaching the Ti surface and
thereby eliminates the oxidation-driven stress.  No delamination
was observed on any device fabricated with the Ti/Ni bilayer
contact.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/5_ti_delamination}
  \caption{Ti contact delamination on (a,\,b)~FAPbBr$_3$ and (c)~CsPbBr$_3$ crystal faces following deposition without a Ni capping layer.}
  \label{fig:ti_delamination}
\end{figure}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Gamma Spectroscopy Setup}
\label{sec:gamma_setup}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Gamma-ray spectroscopy used a $^{137}$Cs source (662~keV) and a
$^{57}$Co source (14, 122, 136~keV).  The readout chain consisted
of a CAEN A1422 charge-sensitive preamplifier connected directly
to the pixel contacts, and a CAEN N6724 Digitizer
16-bit ADC (16,384 channels).  The 10-$\mu$s shaping time
minimises ballistic deficit.  Digitiser settings: threshold
15~channels, trigger hold-off 1500~ns, input rise time 1200~ns,
trapezoid rise time 8~$\mu$s, trapezoid flat-top 5~$\mu$s,
energy fine gain 10.  The mean free path of a 662-keV photon in
CsPbBr$_3$ ($Z_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 45$,
$\rho \approx 4.5$~g\,cm$^{-3}$) is approximately 7~mm; with
crystal thicknesses of 2--3~mm, fewer than 50\% of photons
interact, so geometric detection efficiency was inherently low.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Electrical Characterisation}
\label{sec:cspbbr_electrical}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The CsPbBr$_3$ device exhibited a working leakage current of
315--710~nA at 1000~V reverse bias.  This current overshot its
quasi-steady-state value immediately after the voltage step, then
decayed gradually over 45~minutes to one hour, consistent with
rapid redistribution of mobile halide vacancies followed by slower
re-equilibration of the resulting space-charge distribution.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Gamma Spectroscopy Results}
\label{sec:cspbbr_results}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The CsPbBr$_3$ detector was operated at 1000~V reverse bias for
$^{137}$Cs measurements.  The source count rate was
17.45~counts\,s$^{-1}$ against a background of
9.11~counts\,s$^{-1}$, a statistically significant response to
the 662-keV source.  However, no resolvable photopeak at the
expected 662-keV channel position was observed.  The spectrum
displayed a broad continuum at low channels, consistent with
incomplete charge collection, and a spurious artefact near
channel 16,382 (the ADC maximum), attributed to ADC saturation
from large ion-migration-induced current transients.  A Geant4
Monte Carlo simulation of the crystal geometry confirmed the
features expected for a small-volume Compton spectrometer.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/5_cspbbr_spectrum}
  \caption{CsPbBr$_3$ pulse-height spectrum acquired at 1000~V reverse bias with a $^{137}$Cs source.}
  \label{fig:cspbbr_spectrum}
\end{figure}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:cspbbr_discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The resolution-limiting factor was the large, unstable leakage
current, not the intrinsic charge transport properties of
CsPbBr$_3$.  At 1000~V, the RMS noise generated by 315--710~nA
of leakage current is far larger than the $\sim$0.4~pC signal
charge from a single 662-keV gamma photon, rendering
spectroscopic pulse resolution impossible during the transient
stabilisation period.  Reducing leakage is therefore the primary
engineering challenge, achievable through improved Schottky
barrier quality (lower sputter power, better vacuum) and contact
passivation to prevent the Ti-Br$^-$ chemical reaction documented
in Section~\ref{sec:contact_degradation}.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:fapbbr_discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The FAPbBr$_3$ results constitute a proof-of-concept demonstration
of gamma spectroscopy in a solution-grown halide perovskite crystal.
However, energy resolution remains far below that required for
isotope identification in practical nuclear security applications.
Resolution is limited by: residual leakage-current noise even at
5~nA; incomplete charge collection from carrier trapping analogous
to that described in Section~\ref{sec:mapbi_trapping}; and ion
migration-induced fluctuations in the internal electric field
during acquisition, causing effective detector gain to drift and
broaden spectral features.

The superior performance of FAPbBr$_3$ relative to CsPbBr$_3$
under high-voltage operation is consistent with literature reports
that FA-based perovskites exhibit reduced ion migration rates
compared to Cs-based compositions~\cite{Yuan2016ionmig}: the
larger FA$^+$ cation creates a more tightly packed lattice with
fewer pathways for halide vacancy migration.

\section{MAPbBr$_3$ Gamma-Ray Detector}
\label{sec:mapbbr_issues}
%% ============================================================

MAPbBr$_3$ (methylammonium lead tribromide) single crystals were
processed through the same Au-pixel / Ti/Ni back-face contact
fabrication protocol as CsPbBr$_3$ and FAPbBr$_3$ and were
electrically functional.  However, no resolved gamma photopeak
was obtained from any MAPbBr$_3$ crystal tested, and no
spectroscopic data are presented for this material.  This section
explains why, as the limiting factors are instructive for
understanding the broader requirements for photopeak development
in perovskite gamma detectors.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Crystal Preparation}
\label{sec:mapbbr_fabrication}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

MAPbBr$_3$ single crystals were grown externally and received by
the research group.  Each crystal was inspected under optical
magnification, mechanically planarised using silicon carbide
abrasive paper, and polished with diamond polishing paste to a
10-nm surface finish with hexane-bath rinses between stages.
Contacts were deposited using the Au-pixel / Ti/Ni back-face
protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:cspbbr_fabrication},
which produced adhered, non-delaminated contacts on all
MAPbBr$_3$ crystals processed.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/5_mapbbr_polishing}
  \caption{MAPbBr$_3$ crystal during mechanical polishing preparation.}
  \label{fig:mapbbr_polishing}
\end{figure}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Ion Migration Pulses and the Low-$\mu\tau$ Spectral
            Background}
\label{sec:mapbbr_ionmig_spectrum}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Even with functional contacts, it is unlikely that the
MAPbBr$_3$ crystals available in this work would have yielded
resolved gamma photopeaks.  The limiting factor is crystal
quality, which directly determines the $\mu\tau$ product and
therefore the detector's ability to convert a gamma interaction
into a resolvable pulse.

In a crystal with a low $\mu\tau$ product, carriers generated by
a gamma interaction are captured by deep trap states before they
can traverse the full thickness of the device.  The resulting
pulse height is reduced and varies with interaction depth, since
carriers generated far from the collecting contact must drift
further and are more likely to be trapped before arrival.  The
pulse-height spectrum from such a device is not a sharp photopeak
at the full interaction energy but instead a broad Compton
continuum extending from zero up to some fraction of the full
energy, with no distinguishable photopeak structure,
qualitatively identical to the spectrum observed for CsPbBr$_3$
at moderate bias voltages (Section~\ref{sec:cspbbr_results}).

At higher applied bias a second and more pernicious effect
emerges: ion migration events generate their own large transient
current pulses that are registered by the charge-sensitive
preamplifier and MCA chain as apparent radiation events.  When
mobile halide vacancies redistribute under the applied field,
they produce sudden changes in the space-charge distribution
near the contacts, inducing charge transients on the preamplifier
input that can be orders of magnitude larger than the
$\sim$0.1--0.4~pC signal from a genuine 662-keV gamma
interaction.  These ion migration pulses are not correlated with
any radiation interaction event; they arise purely from ionic
motion within the crystal.  In the pulse-height spectrum they
appear as a large, structureless background at high channel
numbers, effectively burying any genuine gamma signal beneath an
elevated and irreproducible baseline.  This effect was directly
observed in the CsPbBr$_3$ device, where the anomalous artefact
near channel 16,382 of the ADC
(Section~\ref{sec:cspbbr_results}) is a manifestation of exactly
this mechanism: large ion migration-driven current transients
saturating the ADC input.

The practical consequence is that photopeak development in
perovskite gamma detectors requires the simultaneous satisfaction
of two conditions: a $\mu\tau$ product large enough that
radiation-generated carriers can traverse the full crystal
thickness before being trapped
($\mu\tau E / d \gtrsim 1$ in the notation of
Equation~\ref{eq:hecht}), and an ion migration rate low enough
that ionic transient pulses do not dominate the acquired
spectrum.  For the bromide perovskites examined in this work,
FAPbBr$_3$ came closest to meeting both conditions, owing to its
relatively stable leakage current and the reduced ion migration
rate associated with the FA$^+$ sublattice.  The MAPbBr$_3$
crystals, by contrast, did not exhibit sufficient crystal purity
to clear either threshold.  Improving crystal quality,
specifically reducing halide vacancy concentrations through
controlled stoichiometry and inert-atmosphere growth, is
therefore a prerequisite for photopeak development in future
MAPbBr$_3$ devices.

\section{MHyPbCl$_3$ Fast Neutron Detector}
\label{sec:neutron_detector}
%% ============================================================

MHyPbCl$_3$ (methylhydrazinium lead trichloride) represents the
first organic-metal halide perovskite semiconductor evaluated as
a direct-conversion fast neutron detector.  The device uniquely
combines a high hydrogen density comparable to conventional
organic scintillators with the electrical properties of a
high-resistivity semiconductor, enabling direct charge-collection
detection of recoil proton pulses without a separate converter
layer.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Motivation and Material Selection}
\label{sec:neutron_motivation}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Fast neutron detection is challenging because neutrons carry no
charge and cannot interact directly with the electron clouds that
govern detector response.  Instead, fast neutrons are detected via
secondary charged particles, predominantly recoil protons,
produced in elastic scattering collisions with hydrogen nuclei
($^1$H($n$,$n$)$^1$H).  Detection efficiency is proportional to
the material's hydrogen number density $n_H$, which for
conventional hydrogenous semiconductors is typically far too low
to compete with hydrogen-rich organic scintillators such as
stilbene or EJ-276 plastic.

MHyPbCl$_3$ was identified as a candidate because its
methylhydrazinium cation (CH$_3$NH$_2$NH$_2^+$) contributes a
substantial number of hydrogen atoms per formula unit, giving a
calculated $n_H \approx 4.0 \times 10^{22}$~cm$^{-3}$
(Table~\ref{tab:h_density}), comparable to stilbene.  Crucially,
the material also exhibits high resistivity and a large $\mu\tau$
product, making it suitable for direct charge-collection detection
of recoil proton pulses.  An important complication is the
presence of lead ($Z = 82$), a highly efficient photon absorber,
which creates a significant neutron-gamma discrimination challenge
in mixed radiation fields such as those produced by a research
reactor~\cite{Panaccione_NIM_2024}.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Crystal Synthesis and Device Fabrication}
\label{sec:mhy_fabrication}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Methylhydrazinium chloride (MHyCl) was synthesised as the organic
precursor salt: 2.63~mL (50~mmol) of methylhydrazine was diluted
with 5~mL anhydrous IPA, and 100~mL of 0.5~M HCl solution was
added without stirring.  White plate crystals of MHyCl
precipitated spontaneously after overnight storage in inert
atmosphere at room temperature.  The crystals were filtered,
washed with cold IPA, and dried under vacuum, yielding 3.10~g
($\sim$38\%).

MHyPbCl$_3$ single crystals were grown by anti-solvent assisted
crystallisation: 2~mmol each of MHyCl and PbCl$_2$ dissolved in
5~mL DMF / 1~mL DMSO, stirred for 3~h, then filtered through a
0.22-$\mu$m syringe filter.  The solution was transferred to a
20-mL vial placed inside a 100-mL bottle containing 15~mL toluene
as anti-solvent and sealed in a glovebox.  After one week at room
temperature, clear hexagonal MHyPbCl$_3$ crystals formed and
were harvested, dried under vacuum, and surface-polished
immediately prior to electrode deposition.

An ohmic-ohmic contact configuration was adopted.  The lower
crystal face received a 30-nm C$_{60}$ / 6-nm BCP
electron-transport bilayer, followed by an 80-nm Cu contact.
The upper face received a 50-nm Au contact deposited by thermal
evaporation.  The C$_{60}$/BCP bilayer serves simultaneously as
an electron-selective interlayer and interface-state buffer at
the Cu-perovskite junction.  IV characterisation confirmed ohmic,
symmetric behaviour under both polarities.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Electrical Characterisation}
\label{sec:mhy_electrical}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The MHyPbCl$_3$ crystal yielded a resistivity of
$4.43 \times 10^{11}$~$\Omega$\,cm, roughly two orders of
magnitude higher than commercial polycrystalline CZT, placing
it squarely in the range required for high-sensitivity radiation
detectors.  The $\mu\tau$ product extracted by Hecht equation
fitting yielded $\mu\tau = 9.1 \times 10^{-3}$~cm$^2$\,V$^{-1}$,
comparable to high-quality CsPbBr$_3$ single crystals reported
in the literature~\cite{He2019CsPbBr} and approximately an order
of magnitude larger than typical polycrystalline CZT.  This high
$\mu\tau$ reflects extended carrier diffusion lengths achievable
in defect-lean single crystals grown by the slow anti-solvent
method.  IV hysteresis measurements showed a measurable
sweep-versus-hold difference in the low-voltage regime,
indicative of slow ionic relaxation consistent with ion migration.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Digital Pulse Processing Pipeline}
\label{sec:dpp}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Because the radiation-induced charge pulses from the
MHyPbCl$_3$ detector did not exceed approximately 15~mV at the
preamplifier output, they were below the threshold at which
hardware multichannel analyser (MCA) electronics could reliably
discriminate genuine pulses from baseline noise.  A custom
offline digital pulse processing pipeline was therefore developed
in Python to extract pulse-height information from the raw
oscilloscope waveforms.

The processing chain proceeded in four steps.  First, individual
waveforms were loaded from the \texttt{.mat} files written by the
Tektronix MSO54 oscilloscope.  Voltage data were converted to
millivolts and time axes to microseconds to give physically
meaningful units.  Second, each waveform was smoothed using a
Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (LOWESS) algorithm with a
smoothing fraction of 10\% and a single robustness iteration.
LOWESS fits a low-degree polynomial to a sliding local window of
the data, effectively suppressing the high-frequency electronic
noise that dominated the raw preamplifier output while preserving
the slower temporal profile of genuine charge-collection pulses,
whose rise and decay times are governed by carrier transit times
across the crystal and the preamplifier time constant.  Third, a
baseline correction was applied by subtracting the mean of the
first 50 data points of each smoothed waveform, a region prior
to the expected pulse onset, to remove any DC offset that
varied waveform-to-waveform due to amplifier drift or slow ionic
polarisation of the detector.  Fourth, the pulse height was
defined as the maximum absolute value of the baseline-corrected
waveform.  For each waveform the script additionally extracted
the 10\%--90\% rise time and 90\%--10\% decay time of the pulse,
quantities that in principle allow pulse-shape discrimination
between event types, though insufficient statistics prevented
this analysis in the present work.

After pulse-height extraction, pulses with amplitudes below a
threshold of 2~mV were discarded to suppress noise triggers, and
the remaining pulse heights were accumulated into a 50-bin
histogram over the range 0--20~mV to produce the pulse-height
spectrum used for the analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:neutron_results}.  The entire pipeline was
applied independently to each beam configuration subfolder
(shutter open, shutter closed, and beam background), allowing
direct comparison of the pulse-height distributions across
conditions.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:neutron_results}

The MHyPbCl$_3$ detector was operated at reverse bias voltages
of 80~V and 120~V for the FBF measurements.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Discussion: Neutron-Gamma Discrimination and
            Detection Efficiency}
\label{sec:neutron_discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The neutron-gamma discrimination challenge is illustrated by
comparing shutter-open and shutter-closed waveform populations.
Closing the lead gamma shutter reduces the gamma dose rate by
$\sim$300$\times$ (from 15.787~R\,hr$^{-1}$ to
50.525~mR\,hr$^{-1}$) while reducing the fast neutron flux by
only a factor of 35.  If the detector responded purely to
neutrons, the shutter-closed count rate should be $\approx$0.12
times the shutter-open rate.  The observed ratio at 120~V
(1090:1001 after background subtraction) does not show this level
of suppression, indicating the response is dominated by direct
gamma interactions in the Pb sublattice rather than
neutron-induced proton recoil events.  This highlights the
fundamental neutron-gamma discrimination difficulty in a
Pb-containing semiconductor.

An upper bound estimate of fast neutron detection efficiency at
1~MeV neutron energy ($\sigma_{\mathrm{el}} \approx 3$~b) for a
5-mm crystal thickness is
\begin{equation}
  P = 1 - \exp(-n_H \sigma_{\mathrm{el}} t)
    \approx 1 - \exp(-4.0\times10^{22} \times 3\times10^{-24}
             \times 0.5)
    \approx 5.8\%,
  \label{eq:neutron_eff}
\end{equation}
where $t = 0.5$~cm is the path length.  At the shutter-closed
fast neutron flux of $4.3 \times 10^5$~cm$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ and
the $\sim$0.3-cm$^2$ detector area, this implies an expected
proton-recoil interaction rate of $\sim$750~events\,s$^{-1}$,
far larger than the 0.5~counts\,s$^{-1}$ actually observed.  The
discrepancy is primarily due to the Tektronix MSO54 oscilloscope
waveform-write-rate bottleneck: in single-shot waveform
acquisition mode, the maximum write rate was the dominant
constraint on the measurable count rate.  Switching to a hardware
MCA or a fast digitiser in continuous acquisition mode is the
highest-priority hardware improvement identified for future
neutron detection experiments.


%% ============================================================
\section{Ion Migration and Device Stability}
\label{sec:ion_migration_detector}
%% ============================================================

A single overarching materials-physics challenge pervades the
results of Sections~\ref{sec:mapbi_hbn_xray}--\ref{sec:neutron_detector}:
the migration of halide ions under large applied electric fields.
The detector geometry exacerbates this relative to the
photovoltaic geometry in two ways: (1)~the fields required for
efficient charge collection ($\sim$1~V\,$\mu$m$^{-1}$ across
millimetre-scale crystals) are several orders of magnitude larger
than photovoltaic built-in fields; and (2)~these fields must be
sustained continuously for hours during a spectroscopy
acquisition.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Contact Degradation at Elevated Bias}
\label{sec:contact_degradation}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

A distinct degradation mechanism was identified during extended
testing of the FAPbBr$_3$ device: chemical reaction of the
Ti/Ni back-face contact with mobile bromide ions driven toward
that interface under large applied reverse bias.  After a full
day of electrical testing, the Ti contact surface was visually
observed to have changed colour and the metallic Ti layer
appeared to partially recede into or react with the crystal
surface.  Mobile Br$^-$ ions accumulating at the Ti contact
(Ti commonly in the $+3$ oxidation state) can form stable
titanium bromide complexes such as TiBr$_4^{2-}$, consuming
the metal electrode.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/5_contact_degradation}
  \caption{Ti/Ni back-face contact surface of the FAPbBr$_3$ detector after extended high-bias operation.}
  \label{fig:contact_degradation}
\end{figure}

This has profound implications for contact material selection.
Metals that form stable halide compounds under the electrochemical
conditions at a biased contact, titanium being one such metal,
are fundamentally incompatible with sustained high-field
operation in halide perovskite detectors, regardless of the
contact geometry.  The Au pixel contacts showed no evidence of
analogous chemical degradation in any device examined in this
work, consistent with gold's kinetic stability against halide
compound formation under mild electrochemical conditions.
Alternative back-contact candidates identified in the broader
literature include Cr and SnO$_2$, both evaluated on
CsPbBr$_3$ in preliminary experiments but not carried through
to full spectroscopic evaluation within the scope of this work.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Ion Migration Consequences for Spectroscopy}
\label{sec:ion_migration_spectroscopy}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Ion migration impairs spectroscopic performance through three
mechanisms that together set a practical upper bound on the
achievable energy resolution in as-fabricated perovskite single
crystal detectors.

First, ion redistribution under the applied field creates a
non-uniform internal electric field profile.  As mobile anions
accumulate at the anode and cations at the cathode, space-charge
layers form near both contacts.  The resulting space charge adds
to the applied field at one interface and subtracts from it at
the other, so carriers generated at different depths experience
different net fields and have different collection probabilities,
broadening the pulse-height distribution for monoenergetic
radiation events and degrading energy resolution~\cite{He1997CZT}.

Second, the leakage current overshoot upon initial bias
application causes the internal field distribution to evolve
continuously during acquisition.  Since pulse-height peak
position is proportional to instantaneous charge collection
efficiency, the slow field evolution during ion migration causes
effective detector gain to drift, further broadening accumulated
spectral peaks.

Third, on long timescales the accumulated ionic charge at the
interfaces depresses the net bulk electric field, reducing
carrier drift velocity and increasing the probability of trapping
before collection.  The combination of all three effects means
that energy resolution of a perovskite detector operated at high
bias in ambient conditions will inevitably worsen over time, even
if the initial resolution at the moment of bias application is
acceptable.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Mitigation Strategies}
\label{sec:mitigation}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Several mitigation strategies were evaluated or identified in the
course of this work.

\textbf{5F-PEAI surface passivation.}  The most promising
approach demonstrated in this work for thin-film geometries
(Section~\ref{sec:5fpeai_results}).  At 4~mg\,mL$^{-1}$, 5F-PEAI
penetrates grain boundaries and raises the water-penetration
activation energy from $\sim$5~meV to $\sim$120~meV, stabilising
the device to $-16$~V in 40\% RH air.  Extending this passivation
strategy to 3D single crystal systems is a high-priority
direction for future research.

\textbf{Contact material engineering.}  The Ti-Br$^-$ reaction
documented in Section~\ref{sec:contact_degradation} motivates
replacing Ti with metals that do not form stable halide compounds
under detector operating conditions, such as Au, Pt, or
Cr-based multilayers.  Carrier-selective interlayers such as the
C$_{60}$:BCP bilayer used in the MHyPbCl$_3$ device can also
reduce interface recombination velocity and extend the stable
field range.

\textbf{Improved crystal quality.}  Reducing halide vacancy
concentrations through growth conditions that minimise
stoichiometric excess, such as the slow anti-solvent
crystallisation used for MHyPbCl$_3$, or single-crystal
growth under inert atmosphere, is an important near-term research
goal~\cite{Saidaminov2015}.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch5_summary}
%% ============================================================

This chapter described the development and characterisation of
six halide perovskite direct-conversion radiation detectors across
three radiation types: X-rays, gamma rays, and fast neutrons.

The MAPbI$_3$-hBN device (Section~\ref{sec:mapbi_hbn_xray})
demonstrated a monotonically increasing X-ray sensitivity with
applied bias, $0.107$, $0.245$, and
$0.411~\mu$C\,(Gy\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ at 5, 10, and 20~V,
respectively, consistent with the Hecht equation.  Vacuum
testing confirmed moisture-accelerated ion migration as the
principal degradation mechanism, and the device failed
irreversibly after $\sim$5~hours at 400~V.

The 2D PEA$_2$MA$_2$Pb$_3$I$_{10}$ detector with 5F-PEAI
(Section~\ref{sec:2d_perovskite_xray}) demonstrated that
moisture-accelerated instability can be effectively mitigated:
breakdown voltage was raised from $-10$~V to $-16$~V at 40\% RH,
and X-ray sensitivities approaching
$\sim$1000~$\mu$C\,(Gy$_{\mathrm{air}}$\,cm$^2$)$^{-1}$ were achieved
at 4~V bias, among the highest reported for polycrystalline
perovskite detectors of comparable thickness.

The CsPbBr$_3$ device (Section~\ref{sec:cspbbr_gamma}) produced
a statistically significant count-rate response to $^{137}$Cs
(17.45~cps vs.\ 9.11~cps background) at 1000~V, but large
unstable leakage (315--710~nA) generated electronic noise that
prevented resolution of the 662-keV photopeak.

The FAPbBr$_3$ device (Section~\ref{sec:fapbbr_gamma}) achieved
substantially lower operating leakage ($5 \pm 2.5$~nA maximum)
and sustained stable high-voltage operation for over five hours.
A joint $^{137}$Cs/$^{57}$Co acquisition at 1600~V produced
spectral features attributable to both sources, a
proof-of-concept demonstration of gamma spectroscopy in a
solution-grown halide perovskite crystal.

The MAPbBr$_3$ device (Section~\ref{sec:mapbbr_issues}) failed
to produce functional Schottky contacts due to high sputter power
and residual surface contamination, motivating a revised
deposition protocol at reduced sputter power ($\sim$150--200~W)
with in-situ surface cleaning.

MHyPbCl$_3$ (Section~\ref{sec:neutron_detector}) was
characterised as the first organic-metal halide perovskite
semiconductor evaluated in a reactor fast neutron beam.  The
material combines $n_H \approx 4.0 \times 10^{22}$~cm$^{-3}$
(comparable to stilbene) with resistivity
$4.43 \times 10^{11}$~$\Omega$\,cm and
$\mu\tau = 9.1 \times 10^{-3}$~cm$^2$\,V$^{-1}$.  Waveform
data acquired at the OSURR FBF confirmed radiation-induced pulse
activity distinct from beam background; pulse-height spectra were
consistent with the expected proton-recoil energy deposition
distribution from a poly-energetic fast neutron beam.  The
LOWESS-based digital pulse processing pipeline was essential for
extracting these low-amplitude signals ($< 15$~mV) from the raw
oscilloscope waveforms.  Neutron-gamma discrimination remains a
challenge due to the large Pb content, and the oscilloscope
waveform-write-rate bottleneck prevented collection of sufficient
statistics for quantitative efficiency analysis.

Across all six devices, ion migration under applied electric
fields emerged as the central performance-limiting mechanism.
Surface passivation, demonstrated here via 5F-PEAI for
thin-film devices, is identified as the most promising
near-term strategy for extending the operational stability of
perovskite radiation detectors toward the performance levels
required for practical deployment.
