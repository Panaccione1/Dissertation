\chapter{Digital Electronics Verification Methods for Radiation Environments}
\label{ch:digital_electronics}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ch6_intro}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Radiation environments impose two fundamentally distinct threats on
digital electronics: displacement damage and total ionizing dose (TID)
acting as cumulative material-degradation mechanisms, and single-event
effects (SEEs) acting as stochastic, event-driven disturbances.  Of
these, single-event upsets (SEUs) are of particular concern for
SRAM-based field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) because such devices
store both user data and their own circuit definition in volatile
static-RAM cells that are as vulnerable to ionizing particles as any
other SRAM.  Assessing and mitigating this vulnerability requires
purpose-built verification instruments that can observe radiation-induced
state changes in real time, with sufficient state-volume coverage to
yield statistically meaningful upset rates, yet compact enough to deploy
inside a beam facility with limited infrastructure.

This chapter describes the design, implementation, and experimental
results of the Flip-Flop Shift Register (FFSR) test structure used to
evaluate Xilinx Spartan-7 FPGAs under fast neutron irradiation at the
Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR) Fast Neutron Beam
Facility (FNBF).  Section~\ref{sec:ch6_background} reviews relevant
radiation effects in FPGAs.  Section~\ref{sec:ch6_challenges} discusses
verification challenges specific to FPGA-based systems in nuclear
environments.  Section~\ref{sec:ch6_architecture} presents the FFSR RTL
architecture and governing algorithms.  Section~\ref{sec:ch6_test}
describes the test configuration and radiation environment.
Section~\ref{sec:ch6_results} presents the experimental results from two
reactor runs and the intervening overnight monitoring period.
Section~\ref{sec:ch6_discussion} discusses the observed upset mechanisms
and their implications, and Section~\ref{sec:ch6_summary} summarises the
principal findings.

\section{Radiation Effects in FPGAs}
\label{sec:ch6_background}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

SRAM-based FPGAs are widely used in nuclear instrumentation and
space-borne electronics because of their reconfigurability, high logic
density, and short design cycles.  However, their reliance on SRAM
storage cells for both configuration memory and user state makes them
susceptible to a range of radiation-induced failure modes.

\textbf{Single-Event Upsets.}  An SEU occurs when a charged particle
deposits sufficient energy in an SRAM cell to flip its stored bit.  In
the user data fabric, an SEU manifests as a transient or persistent
logical error in the circuit under test.  In the configuration SRAM,
the consequence is more severe: a bit flip can alter LUT contents,
routing multiplexers, embedded constant strings, or timing-control
registers, effectively changing the implemented circuit itself.  Because
the configuration SRAM is not typically protected by on-chip error
correction in commercial devices, such upsets persist until the device
is power-cycled or a scrubbing operation rewrites the affected cell.

\textbf{Single-Event Latch-up.}  High-energy particle strikes can
trigger parasitic bipolar structures in bulk CMOS, creating a
low-impedance path between supply and ground.  The resulting latch-up
condition draws large currents, halts device operation, and can cause
permanent damage if not cleared by a prompt power cycle.  The
probability of latch-up increases with particle flux and LET.

\textbf{Total Ionizing Dose Effects.}  Prolonged irradiation builds up
trapped charge in gate oxides and field oxides, increasing leakage
current, shifting threshold voltages, and ultimately degrading
transistor drive strength.  TID effects accumulate monotonically with
fluence and manifest at the system level as a gradual increase in supply
current drawn by the device.

\textbf{Displacement Damage.}  Fast neutrons displace lattice atoms
through nuclear recoil, creating interstitial-vacancy pairs that act as
recombination centres and carrier traps.  In CMOS logic this mechanism
is generally less significant than in minority-carrier devices (e.g.,
solar cells or bipolar transistors), but at high fast-neutron fluences
it contributes to parametric degradation.

\section{FPGA Verification Challenges in Nuclear Systems}
\label{sec:ch6_challenges}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Several practical constraints complicate the experimental characterisation
of FPGA upset susceptibility in nuclear beam facilities.

\textbf{State-volume coverage.}  An SEU cross-section measurement
requires observing a statistically significant number of events.  The
cross-section for a single memory cell is of order $10^{-14}$ to
$10^{-12}$~cm$^2$, so at a flux of $10^5$--$10^7$~n/cm$^2$/s a single
cell would require impractically long exposures to accumulate even one
event on average.  Maximising the monitored state population is
therefore essential.

\textbf{Observability without intrusion.}  Reading every flip-flop
off-chip individually during irradiation is infeasible for large state
populations and introduces additional wiring susceptible to radiation
and noise.  A compact on-chip checker that compresses the health of
the state array into a small digital flag is preferable.

\textbf{Configuration vs.\ data upsets.}  Any experimental design must
be capable of distinguishing a user-data upset from a configuration
memory upset.  The two mechanisms require different mitigation
strategies: data-path redundancy (DMR/TMR) addresses the former, while
periodic configuration scrubbing addresses the latter.

\textbf{Telemetry reliability.}  The UART or other communication
channel used to export results is itself implemented in FPGA resources
and therefore subject to the same configuration upsets.  A robust
transmitter design reduces the risk that the observability channel
itself is corrupted during an experiment.

\section{FFSR RTL Architecture}
\label{sec:ch6_architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{System-Level Overview}
\label{subsec:ch6_arch_overview}

The FFSR test structure is an FPGA-resident self-test instrument
designed to address the observability and state-volume challenges
described in Section~\ref{sec:ch6_challenges}.  Its purpose is to
instantiate a large, controllable population of sequential elements,
exercise those elements with known scan/capture behaviour, and report
any deviation from the expected internal state in real time.  The design
contains four cooperating subsystems:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{top-level experiment controller}
          (\texttt{top\_selftest\_100mhz\_dbg}) that generates experiment
          timing, reset sequences, capture windows, and telemetry cadence.

    \item A \textbf{scan-farm fabric} (\texttt{ff\_scanfarm} /
          \texttt{ff\_scanfarm\_sig}) that instantiates many shift-register
          chains in parallel, scaling the monitored state volume.

    \item A \textbf{signature checker} that computes an expected compact
          signature from the chain contents and compares it against the
          observed signature to produce live and latched error flags.

    \item A \textbf{UART telemetry path} that serialises concise status
          words for logging on a host computer, enabling offline
          correlation with beam current, fluence, and shutter state.
\end{enumerate}

Rather than streaming every monitored bit off chip, the design compresses
the health of a large state space into a smaller set of mismatch
indicators and counters.  This separation of upset \emph{occurrence}
from complete state readout enables larger monitored populations and
simpler experimental integration while still supporting controlled
injection tests and persistent error latching.

\subsubsection{top\_selftest\_100mhz\_dbg}

The \texttt{top\_selftest\_100mhz\_dbg} module is the supervisory block
of the FFSR experiment.  It establishes the operating clock domain,
applies reset, sequences the scan and capture phases, clears latched
errors when requested, and routes status information into the UART
telemetry path.  In a typical implementation, operation is divided into
repeated frames or epochs: the design allows the farm to evolve or hold
state, then enables capture, then enables scan shifting, then examines
mismatch outputs, and finally serialises the results.

\subsubsection{ff\_scanfarm and ff\_scanfarm\_sig}

The \texttt{ff\_scanfarm} module acts as a named interface to the
monitored sequential fabric.  In the wrapper variant used in this
project, it preserves a stable external interface while instantiating
the more robust internal implementation \texttt{ff\_scanfarm\_sig}.
This decouples the project hierarchy from changes in the checker
implementation.  The key outputs are a real-time mismatch indicator,
\texttt{error\_live}, and a sticky fault memory, \texttt{error\_any}.

The scan-farm core is parameterised by the number of parallel chains
$C$ and the number of bits per chain $B$, giving a total monitored
state volume of:
\begin{equation}
    N_\text{state} = C \times B.
    \label{eq:nstate}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{scan\_chain Primitive}

At the lowest level, the \texttt{scan\_chain} logic implements the
per-stage shift-register recurrence.  A chain receives a serial input
bit, shifts stored data through successive registers when
\texttt{scan\_en} is asserted, and presents the final stage at
\texttt{scan\_out}.  An optional injection feature can flip a selected
stage, allowing the checker path to be validated without radiation.
Because the chain state is deterministic under commanded scan and
capture sequences, any uncommanded state change is detectable through
signature mismatch.

\subsection{Core Algorithms}
\label{subsec:ch6_algorithms}

\subsubsection{Shift-Register State Update}

Let $q_{c,i}[k]$ denote the state of stage $i$ in chain $c$ at clock
step $k$, where $i = 0, 1, \ldots, B{-}1$ and $c = 0, 1, \ldots,
C{-}1$.  During scan mode the chain obeys the standard shift recurrence:
\begin{align}
    q_{c,0}[k+1]   &= u_c[k], \label{eq:scan_in} \\
    q_{c,i}[k+1]   &= q_{c,i-1}[k], \quad i > 0, \label{eq:scan_shift}
\end{align}
where $u_c[k]$ is the applied serial scan input for chain $c$.  In hold
mode, $q_{c,i}[k+1] = q_{c,i}[k]$.

\subsubsection{Optional Upset Injection for Verification}

To validate the detector path without radiation exposure, a controlled
injection can be applied at tap $t_\text{inj}$:
\begin{equation}
    q_{c,\,t_\text{inj}}[k+1] =
        q^{\,\text{nom}}_{c,\,t_\text{inj}}[k+1]
        \oplus \, \text{inj}[k],
    \label{eq:injection}
\end{equation}
where $\text{inj}[k]$ is asserted only during a commanded test window
and $q^\text{nom}$ denotes the nominal update in the absence of
injection.

\subsubsection{Signature Computation and Mismatch Detection}

A compact checker is formed by reducing chain contents into parity-like
signatures.  The signature for chain $c$ is:
\begin{equation}
    s_c[k] = \bigoplus_{i=0}^{B-1} q_{c,i}[k].
    \label{eq:sig}
\end{equation}
The fabric-level signature vector is $\mathbf{S}[k] = [s_0[k],\,
s_1[k],\, \ldots,\, s_{C-1}[k]]$.  The instantaneous mismatch flag is:
\begin{equation}
    e_\text{live}[k] =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if } \mathbf{S}_\text{obs}[k] \neq \mathbf{S}_\text{exp}[k], \\
        0 & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:elive}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Sticky Error Memory}

A sticky fault flag preserves the occurrence of any prior mismatch even
if the chain later returns to agreement:
\begin{equation}
    e_\text{any}[k+1] =
        \bigl(e_\text{any}[k] \;\vee\; e_\text{live}[k]\bigr)
        \;\wedge\; \overline{\text{clear\_errors}[k]}.
    \label{eq:eany}
\end{equation}
This form is essential in beam testing because transient upsets are not
lost between telemetry packets.

\subsubsection{PRBS31 Recurrence}

The \texttt{prbs31} module generates a pseudo-random binary sequence
using a 31-bit linear feedback shift register with the primitive
polynomial $p(z) = z^{31} + z^{28} + 1$.  Using zero-based indexing
with $x_{30}$ as the most-significant stage, the feedback bit is:
\begin{equation}
    f[k] = x_{30}[k] \oplus x_{27}[k],
    \label{eq:prbs_feedback}
\end{equation}
and the next state is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}[k+1] = \{x_{29}[k],\, x_{28}[k],\, \ldots,\,
                          x_0[k],\, f[k]\}.
    \label{eq:prbs_update}
\end{equation}
Provided the all-zero state is avoided, this recurrence cycles through
$2^{31} - 1$ non-zero states before repeating, yielding strong
transition density and good statistical coverage for stressing the
sequential fabric.

\subsubsection{UART Frame Formation}

For an 8-bit telemetry byte $D = [d_0, d_1, \ldots, d_7]$, the UART
transmitter outputs:
\begin{equation}
    \text{TX frame} = [0,\; d_0,\; d_1,\; \ldots,\; d_7,\; 1],
    \label{eq:uart_frame}
\end{equation}
where 0 is the start bit and 1 is the stop bit.  The nominal bit-period
divider is:
\begin{equation}
    N_\text{baud} = \left\lfloor\frac{f_\text{clk}}{f_\text{baud}}
                    \right\rceil,
    \label{eq:baud}
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor\cdot\rceil$ denotes rounding to the nearest integer.  The
\texttt{uart\_tx\_robust} variant extends the baseline transmitter with
stronger start-request handling, safer state transitions, and additional
protection against malformed back-to-back frames under transient control
behaviour.

\subsection{Radiation Environment}
\label{subsec:ch6_radiation}

The test was conducted at the OSURR Fast Neutron Beam Facility over two
days using distinct beam configurations that produced substantially
different neutron flux and fluence levels.

\textbf{Day~1 (gamma shutter closed, January~27).}  The reactor
operated at approximately 300~kW (steady-state), reached within
$\sim$10~minutes of start-up and sustained for approximately 4.7~hours.
The fast neutron flux (steady-state) was approximately $2 \times 10^5$
n/cm$^2$/s; the thermal component was approximately one order of
magnitude lower.  Total accumulated fast neutron fluence was
approximately $6$--$7 \times 10^9$~n/cm$^2$.  The fast and total
fluence curves were nearly coincident throughout, confirming fast-neutron
dominance.

\textbf{Day~2 (gamma shutter open, January~28).}  Reactor power
remained at approximately 300~kW, but the open-shutter configuration
produced a fast neutron flux of approximately $1.5 \times 10^7$
n/cm$^2$/s---roughly two orders of magnitude higher than Day~1.  The
thermal flux was approximately $6 \times 10^6$~n/cm$^2$/s (within a
factor of 2--3 of the fast flux).  Total accumulated fast neutron
fluence was approximately $8$--$9 \times 10^{11}$~n/cm$^2$, also
approximately two orders of magnitude greater than Day~1.

When the lead shutter is closed, the gamma-ray content of the beam is reduced by a factor of 311 relative to the fully open configuration, while fast neutrons are simultaneously attenuated by a factor of 58.
Under these conditions, the steady-state fast neutron flux ($E_n > 0.5$~eV) at the beam collimator exit is approximately $2.6 \times 10^5$~n/cm$^2$/s, while the thermal neutron flux ($E_n < 0.5$~eV) is approximately $2.5 \times 10^4$~n/cm$^2$/s---roughly one order of magnitude lower than the fast component.
The residual gamma dose rate with the shutter closed is approximately 50~mR/h.
The neutron energy spectrum of the FNBF beam, characterised using the SAND-II and STAYSL unfolding techniques with Cu, Au, and Ni metal wire neutron activation, spans thermal energies through the fast fission spectrum, but the fast and epi-thermal components dominate the integrated flux~\cite{Panaccione2024}.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:ch6_results}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Baseline Test (January~26, Out of Beam)}
\label{subsec:ch6_baseline}

Prior to transport to the reactor facility, all five devices were
operated at ambient background radiation levels to establish a baseline
and to confirm correct data acquisition system operation.  FFSR1 began
logging at 21:21 on January~26; FFSR2--5 began at 22:41.  All devices
reported \texttt{CNT:00000000} continuously throughout the baseline
period, confirming zero false positives from the test setup and
demonstrating that the DMR detection logic was functioning correctly.

Logging on all five devices terminated simultaneously at approximately
22:59 due to a Windows Update reboot on the acquisition PC.  This was
not a device failure.  After the reboot, logging was not restarted until
the devices were at the reactor facility the following morning.  Future
tests should disable automatic OS updates on the acquisition PC for the
duration of the experiment.

\subsection{Day~1 Reactor Run (January~27)}
\label{subsec:ch6_day1}

Logging resumed at the reactor facility at approximately 11:42 on
January~27 for all five devices.  The reactor reached full power within
the first 10--15~minutes.  All devices monitored continuously through
reactor shutdown at approximately 16:34, providing approximately five
hours of reactor run data.

\subsubsection{Upset Count Results}

Zero SEUs were detected on any of the five devices throughout the entire

\subsubsection{FFSR1 UART Format Change---Candidate Configuration Memory SEU}
\label{subsec:ch6_ffsr1_day1}

At approximately 12:03 on January~27, the UART output format on FFSR1
changed from \texttt{CNT:00000000} to \texttt{CNZ0000}.  The label
changed from \texttt{CNT} to \texttt{CNZ}, the colon delimiter
disappeared, and the counter field width reduced from 8 hex digits to 4.
This format change persisted for the remainder of the run; the upset
count remained zero throughout in both formats; and FFSR2--5 showed no
similar change.

This behaviour is consistent with a single-event upset in the FPGA
configuration memory (SRAM) rather than in the user data path.  The
Spartan-7 is an SRAM-based FPGA, meaning the design implementation---LUT
contents, routing resources, and embedded string constants---is stored in
volatile SRAM configuration bits as susceptible to neutron-induced upsets
as any other SRAM.  A bit flip in one or more configuration cells
controlling the UART formatting logic could plausibly alter the output
label and field width in exactly the structured manner observed.

This event is significant for two reasons.  First, the DMR logic
correctly protected the user flip-flop data path and reported zero upsets
throughout, but it does not protect the configuration memory; the
observation highlights that data-path redundancy alone is insufficient
for SRAM-based FPGAs in radiation environments.  Second, the single
affected device (out of five) is consistent with the stochastic nature
of SEUs at the Day~1 fluence level.

\subsubsection{FFSR3 and FFSR4 Early Dropouts}

FFSR4 ceased transmitting at approximately 07:57 ($\sim$36~minutes into
the run) and FFSR3 at approximately 08:10 ($\sim$49~minutes in).  Both
devices stopped cleanly with no error messages; data simply ended
mid-run and neither device recovered.  Two plausible mechanisms are
consistent with these observations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiation-induced latch-up.}  A high-energy neutron
    interaction could trigger a latch-up condition in the FPGA I/O or
    core circuitry, causing the device to lock up and halt UART output.
    Latch-up typically requires a power cycle to recover and can cause
    permanent damage if not cleared promptly.

    \item \textbf{Critical configuration memory upset.}  A severe or
    multi-bit upset in a critical portion of the configuration memory
    (e.g., clock management or I/O control logic) could halt device
    operation entirely, even without a true latch-up.
\end{itemize}

The PSU current data
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch6_day2_cmod_part1}) shows step increases at
approximately 2\,800~s and 5\,000~s into the run.  FFSR4 stopped at
approximately 2\,160~s and FFSR3 at approximately 2\,940~s, placing both
dropouts near the step increase at $\sim$2\,800~s.  An abrupt change in
aggregate board current at this time is consistent with a latch-up event
altering one or both devices' current draw.  These early dropouts
represent the most severe functional failures observed during the
two-day campaign and are consistent with the two-orders-of-magnitude
increase in fast neutron flux on Day~2.

\subsubsection{FFSR1 End-of-Run Burst Event and Confirmed DMR Upset}
\label{subsec:ch6_ffsr1_day2}

The most significant finding of the entire test campaign occurred on
FFSR1 at approximately 15:54:44 on January~28.  Under normal operation,
FFSR1 transmitted one \texttt{CNZ} output line every $\sim$5~seconds.
At 15:54:44, the device abruptly began transmitting at an extremely high
rate---approximately 400~readings within a 2-second window, roughly
500$\times$ the normal output rate.  Within this burst the following
anomalies were observed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Multiple corrupted character substitutions:
          \texttt{cNZ0000} (lowercase `c'), \texttt{CLZ0000}
          (`L' for `N'), \texttt{KNZ0000} (`K' for `C'),
          \texttt{CN[0000} (`[' for `Z').
    \item A truncated output: \texttt{CNZ0} (partial transmission).
    \item A non-zero upset count: \texttt{CNZ0800}
          (hexadecimal \texttt{0x0800} = 2\,048 decimal).
\end{itemize}

The value \texttt{CNZ0800} is the \emph{first and only non-zero DMR
upset count recorded across the entire two-day test campaign},
representing a confirmed detection of one or more single-event upsets in
the FFSR user data path by the DMR comparator logic.

The burst event itself---the $\sim$500$\times$ increase in UART
transmission rate---is strongly indicative of a second configuration
memory SEU on FFSR1, this time affecting the UART baud rate generation
logic.  The baud rate divider is implemented in FPGA configuration
resources; a bit flip in the timing register can reduce the
inter-character delay to near zero, producing exactly the rapid-fire
transmission pattern observed.  The coincidence of the baud rate upset,
the character corruption events, and the non-zero DMR count within a
2-second window is consistent with a high-rate SEU cluster---multiple
configuration and data memory upsets occurring in rapid succession near
the end of the reactor run when cumulative fluence was at its maximum.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:ch6_discussion}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{SEU Sensitivity of the DMR Design}
\label{subsec:ch6_disc_seu}

The absence of any DMR-detected upsets across all five devices at the
Day~1 fast neutron fluence of approximately $6$--$7 \times 10^9$
n/cm$^2$ indicates either that the DMR design successfully masked data
path upsets that would otherwise be detectable, or that the Spartan-7
shift-register fabric did not experience a sufficient number of upset
events to register a DMR mismatch at this fluence level.  To distinguish
between these interpretations, a companion test without DMR protection
(e.g., a bare shift-register comparison) would be needed to establish
the intrinsic SEU cross-section of the Spartan-7 fabric under this beam
condition.

On Day~2, the first confirmed DMR data path SEU (counter value 2\,048)
was observed on FFSR1 at the highest fluence level of the campaign
($\sim$$8$--$9 \times 10^{11}$~n/cm$^2$ total fast fluence).  The
two-orders-of-magnitude increase in flux and fluence from Day~1 to Day~2
is qualitatively consistent with the transition from zero detectable
data-path upsets on Day~1 to device failures, latch-up events, and
confirmed DMR detection on Day~2.

\subsection{Configuration Memory Vulnerability}
\label{subsec:ch6_disc_cfg}

The FFSR1 observations across both days provide clear evidence of
configuration memory SEUs manifesting as persistent, structured changes
in device behaviour.  The Day~1 UART label change
(Section~\ref{subsec:ch6_ffsr1_day1}) and the Day~2 burst event
(Section~\ref{subsec:ch6_ffsr1_day2}) both represent configuration memory
upsets whose effects persisted until the device was power-cycled,
consistent with the volatile SRAM nature of the Spartan-7 configuration
store.  These observations highlight that SRAM-based FPGAs have two
distinct radiation vulnerability mechanisms: upsets in the user data
registers (addressed by DMR/TMR) and upsets in the configuration SRAM
(addressed by periodic scrubbing).  Radiation-hardened designs intended
for extended exposure environments must address both.

\subsection{TID Effects in PSU Current Data}
\label{subsec:ch6_disc_tid}

The PSU current monitoring data provides a consistent and clear picture
of TID accumulation across the two days.  The Day~1 current profile was
flat throughout ($\sim$0.284~A), indicating negligible TID-induced
leakage at the Day~1 fluence level.  In contrast, the Day~2 current
profile exhibited a steady monotonic increase of approximately 12.5\%
over the approximately 7-hour run.  This is consistent with TID effects
in the FPGA and support circuitry at the much higher Day~2 fluence.
The step increases in the Day~2 current profile near 2\,800~s and
5\,000~s provide independent corroborating evidence for latch-up events
at the times of the FFSR4 and FFSR3 dropouts.

\subsection{Implications for FPGA Radiation Hardening}
\label{subsec:ch6_disc_hardening}

Taken together, the results of this two-day test campaign demonstrate
several conclusions relevant to the use of SRAM-based FPGAs in fast
neutron environments.

First, at moderate fluence levels ($\sim$$10^9$~n/cm$^2$), configuration
memory SEUs can occur before data-path SEUs are detectable at the DMR
counter level, suggesting that configuration scrubbing should be
considered even in designs that rely on DMR or TMR for data-path
protection.

Second, at higher fluence levels ($\sim$$10^{11}$~n/cm$^2$ and above),
latch-up, multiple simultaneous SEUs, and TID-induced leakage all become
significant concerns that require active mitigation strategies including
latch-up detection and recovery circuitry, TID-hardened component
selection, and TID-aware power budget margins.

Third, the FFSR architecture proved effective as a radiation test
instrument.  The DMR comparator correctly identified the single confirmed
data-path upset event while maintaining consistent zero-count output
throughout normal operation, and the UART telemetry path provided a
reliable real-time window into device state for the duration of both
reactor runs.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch6_summary}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter presented the design and experimental results of the FFSR
DMR irradiation test conducted at the OSURR Fast Neutron Beam Facility
on January~26--28, 2026.  The principal findings are as follows.

\textbf{Day~1 (gamma shutter closed, $\sim$$6$--$7 \times 10^9$
n/cm$^2$ fast fluence).}  Zero DMR-detected SEUs across all five devices
during approximately five hours of irradiation.  FFSR1 exhibited a UART
output format change consistent with a configuration memory SEU while
the DMR upset counter remained zero.  No measurable TID effects were
observed in the PSU current data.

\textbf{Overnight (out of beam, January~27--28).}  Zero upsets across
all five devices during approximately 14.5~hours of ambient monitoring.
The FFSR1 \texttt{CNZ} format persisted through all logging restarts,
confirming a persistent SRAM hardware state change rather than a
software artefact.

\textbf{Day~2 (gamma shutter open, $\sim$$8$--$9 \times 10^{11}$
n/cm$^2$ fast fluence).}  FFSR3 and FFSR4 experienced early functional
dropouts at approximately 36 and 49~minutes respectively, consistent
with radiation-induced latch-up supported by the PSU current data.
FFSR2 and FFSR5 completed the full run with zero DMR upsets.  FFSR1
experienced a severe multi-SEU cluster event near the end of the run,
producing the first and only confirmed non-zero DMR upset count of the
campaign (\texttt{CNZ0800}, 0\texttt{x}0800 = 2\,048) along with a
second configuration memory SEU affecting the UART baud rate.  PSU
current monitoring showed a sustained $\sim$12.5\% increase over the
Day~2 run, consistent with TID-induced leakage current accumulation.

Overall, these results demonstrate that SRAM-based FPGAs operating in
this fast neutron environment are susceptible to configuration memory
SEUs at moderate fluence levels, and to data-path SEUs, latch-up, and
TID degradation at higher fluence levels.  The FFSR DMR architecture
successfully fulfilled its design objective of detecting data-path upsets
while operating reliably throughout the test campaign, and highlights the
need for configuration memory scrubbing and latch-up recovery circuitry
for SRAM-based FPGAs intended for extended operation in radiation
environments.
