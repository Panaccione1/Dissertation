\chapter{Background: Radiation Effects on Digital Electronics and
         FPGA-Based Instrumentation}
\label{ch:FPGA_background}

The deployment of field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) in nuclear
instrumentation and control (I\&C) systems represents a convergence of
two historically separate engineering disciplines: the radiation effects
community, which has characterized and mitigated the response of
microelectronics to ionizing radiation primarily in the context of
space and defense applications, and the nuclear industry, which is
confronting the simultaneous pressures of fleet aging, digital
modernization, and cost reduction described in Chapter~1.
This chapter provides the technical background required to understand
the FPGA-based monitoring systems developed in Chapters~\ref{ch:digital_electronics} and~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark}.
It begins with the radiation environments of concern, then develops the
physical mechanisms by which ionizing particles damage or disturb
microelectronic devices, surveys the taxonomy of single-event effects
(SEEs) with emphasis on those most relevant to SRAM-based FPGAs, describes
the fault-tolerance architectures used to mitigate those effects, and
concludes with the testing methodologies used to characterize and qualify
devices for radiation environments.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Radiation Environments for Digital Electronics}
\label{sec:rad_environments}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Digital electronics encounter ionizing radiation in three principal
deployment contexts relevant to this work: the near-Earth space
environment, the nuclear reactor plant environment, and the terrestrial
cosmic-ray environment at ground level.  Each environment differs in
particle species, energy spectra, and dose rates, and each imposes
distinct qualification requirements.

\subsection{The Space Radiation Environment}

The space environment exposes electronics to three primary sources of
energetic particles.  \emph{Trapped protons and electrons} in the Van
Allen belts (primarily 1--10~MeV protons in the inner belt, up to
several hundred MeV in the South Atlantic Anomaly) produce continuous
total ionizing dose accumulation and displacement damage that
degrades device performance over mission lifetime~\cite{petersen2011single}.
\emph{Galactic cosmic rays} (GCRs) are fully ionized nuclei spanning
the entire periodic table, with energies from tens of MeV/nucleon to
above 10$^{20}$~eV; they are isotropic, difficult to shield, and
responsible for the bulk of single-event effects at high
altitude~\cite{ziegler1996terrestrial}.  \emph{Solar energetic particles}
(SEPs), produced during coronal mass ejections and solar flares, deliver
intense but episodic fluences of protons (up to hundreds of MeV) and
heavy ions that can cause acute upsets far exceeding the GCR
background~\cite{ecoffet2013inorbit}.

The cumulative radiation dose received by a spacecraft depends strongly
on orbit, shielding, and mission duration.  Low-Earth orbit (LEO)
satellites at International Space Station altitude ($\sim$400~km,
51.6$^\circ$ inclination) accumulate roughly 5--20~krad(Si)/year
behind 100~mil aluminum shielding, while geostationary (GEO) and
medium-Earth orbit (MEO) satellites may see 30--100~krad(Si)/year
due to prolonged residence in the Van Allen
belts~\cite{schwank2013radiation}.  Qualification standards such
as MIL-STD-883 and ESA ESCC 22900 require demonstration of margin
over these mission doses before flight hardware can be approved.

\subsection{The Nuclear Plant Radiation Environment}

Within an operating nuclear power plant, electronics installed in
or near the reactor building are exposed to a mixed neutron-gamma
field whose intensity depends strongly on location.  In-core
instrumentation must withstand integrated fast-neutron fluences
of $10^{17}$--$10^{19}$~n/cm$^2$ and total gamma doses of
$10^6$--$10^8$~rad over the reactor lifetime.  Ex-core
instrumentation in the primary containment sees lower but still
significant fluences of $10^{13}$--$10^{16}$~n/cm$^2$ and
gamma doses of $10^4$--$10^6$~rad~\cite{iaea2016design}.  Safety
I\&C systems in the auxiliary building and control room operate
at much lower dose rates (typically well below 1~rad/year) but
must maintain functionality under design-basis accident conditions
that could spike the local dose rate by several orders of
magnitude for hours to days~\cite{nrc1997digital}.

The neutron fluence in reactor environments produces displacement
damage in semiconductor lattices that is qualitatively distinct
from ionizing dose effects: energetic neutrons displace silicon
atoms from lattice sites, creating vacancy-interstitial pairs and
vacancy clusters that act as generation-recombination centers,
increase leakage current, reduce minority carrier lifetime, and
degrade transistor gain~\cite{messenger1992effects}.  SRAM-based
FPGAs, whose configuration memory cells rely on stable stored
charge, are particularly sensitive to neutron-induced displacement
damage in the pass transistors that gate cell access.

\subsection{The Terrestrial Cosmic-Ray Environment}

At sea level, the secondary cosmic-ray flux consists primarily of
muons, with a smaller but device-relevant component of high-energy
neutrons (``spallation'' neutrons) produced by hadronic showers in
the upper atmosphere.  The terrestrial fast-neutron flux at sea level
is approximately $1.3 \times 10^{-2}$~n/cm$^2$/s for neutrons above
10~MeV, increasing by roughly a factor of three per kilometer of
altitude~\cite{normand1996single,ziegler1996terrestrial}.  Although
this flux is orders of magnitude lower than in accelerator or reactor
environments, it is ubiquitous and non-negligible for high-density
memories operating in safety-critical ground applications.  The
JEDEC standard JESD89A quantifies the terrestrial soft-error rate
environment and defines the reference neutron spectrum at sea level
against which device qualification measurements are
normalized~\cite{jedec2006jesd89a}.

\section{Total Ionizing Dose Effects}
\label{sec:TID}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Total ionizing dose (TID) refers to the cumulative energy deposited
in a material by ionizing radiation, principally energetic electrons,
protons, and gamma photons, integrated over the operating lifetime
of the device.  In metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) structures, which
form the transistor basis of all modern digital FPGAs, the primary
site of TID damage is the gate oxide and the field oxide used to
isolate adjacent transistors.

\subsection{Charge Trapping in Gate Oxides}

When ionizing radiation passes through a SiO$_2$ gate oxide, it
generates electron-hole pairs with a yield proportional to the local
electric field and the oxide thickness.  Electrons, which have high
mobility in SiO$_2$ ($\mu_e \approx 20$~cm$^2$/V$\cdot$s), sweep
out of the oxide rapidly under the applied gate field on nanosecond
timescales.  Holes, with a mobility orders of magnitude lower,
undergo a dispersive hopping transport toward the Si/SiO$_2$
interface, where a fraction become trapped at pre-existing or
radiation-induced oxide trap sites~\cite{oldham2003total}.  The
resulting positive oxide trapped charge shifts the transistor
threshold voltage $V_T$ in the negative direction (for n-channel
MOSFETs), eventually causing the device to conduct when it should
be off, a failure mode known as threshold-voltage
rolloff~\cite{fleetwood2013total}.

In parallel, radiation generates interface trap states at the
Si/SiO$_2$ boundary by breaking Si-H bonds.  These interface traps
contribute both a fixed charge and a frequency-dependent
recombination current that degrades transistor transconductance,
increases $1/f$ noise, and reduces drive current.  Unlike oxide
trapped charge, which anneals partially at room temperature,
interface traps are thermally stable on device-lifetime
timescales~\cite{oldham2003total}.

\subsection{TID Effects in Modern FPGAs}

The susceptibility of a CMOS process to TID is strongly dependent
on gate oxide thickness: thinner oxides, which dominate in
sub-micron nodes, trap fewer holes because the thin-oxide regime
favors rapid recombination before hole transport can occur.
A 65-nm process FPGA may tolerate 300--500~krad(Si) before
functional failure, compared to 30--100~krad(Si) for older
1-$\mu$m process devices~\cite{schwank2013radiation}.  However,
the isolation structures (shallow trench isolation, STI) in modern
processes are relatively thick and remain a TID-sensitive element,
causing leakage current between adjacent transistors at doses that
do not yet affect the gate oxides.

For the nuclear instrumentation context, the TID tolerance of
commercial SRAM FPGAs in the control room or auxiliary building
is generally adequate without special hardening, as cumulative
doses over a plant lifetime at those locations are well below the
few-krad level~\cite{ANS_ICModernization_2025}.  The primary concern
for FPGAs in nuclear plants is therefore not TID but the stochastic
single-event effects described in the next section, which occur at
arbitrarily low dose rates and cannot be mitigated by simply
limiting exposure time.

\section{Single-Event Effects: Physical Mechanisms}
\label{sec:SEE_physics}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Ion Track Formation and Charge Collection}

A single-event effect (SEE) is a functional or physical disturbance
in a microelectronic device caused by the passage of a single
energetic particle.  The mechanism begins with the energy deposition
of the incident particle along its track through the semiconductor.
Charged particles, heavy ions, protons, and the recoil nuclei from
neutron elastic scattering or nuclear reactions, lose energy
primarily through Coulomb interactions with the semiconductor
electrons, creating a dense column of electron-hole plasma along
the track.

The key parameter governing SEE sensitivity is the linear energy
transfer (LET), defined as the energy deposited per unit path length:
\begin{equation}
  \text{LET} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dE}{dx}
  \quad \left[\text{MeV}\cdot\text{cm}^2\text{/mg}\right],
  \label{eq:LET}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the material density.  For a silicon target, an LET
of 1~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg deposits approximately $1.6 \times 10^{-13}$~J
per micron of path length, producing roughly 10$^4$ electron-hole
pairs per micron.  The LET of a given ion depends on its atomic number
$Z$, mass $A$, and kinetic energy; heavy ions near the middle of the
periodic table (e.g., Fe, Kr, Xe, Bi) with energies of tens of
MeV/nucleon achieve the highest LETs, up to
$\sim$100~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg in silicon~\cite{petersen2011single}.

Following the primary ionization event, the excess carriers are
collected at the nearest reverse-biased junction by two mechanisms
illustrated in Figs.~\ref{fig:funneling_conc} and \ref{fig:funneling_pot}.
\emph{Drift collection} occurs because the high carrier density
temporarily collapses the depletion region and extends the electric
field deep into the substrate, a phenomenon known as
\emph{funneling}, sweeping charge toward the junction on picosecond
timescales~\cite{dodd2003basic}.  \emph{Diffusion collection} then
operates on slower nanosecond timescales as carriers generated
outside the funnel diffuse through the neutral substrate and are
eventually collected.  The total collected charge $Q_{\mathrm{coll}}$
is determined by the integral of the current transient at the
junction and must exceed the critical charge $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ of
the struck node to cause a SEE.

\subsection{The SEE Sensitivity Figure of Merit: Cross-Section}

The probability that a particle causes a SEE is characterized by
the device SEE cross-section $\sigma$, defined as the number of
events observed per unit incident particle fluence:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(L) = \frac{N_{\mathrm{events}}}{\Phi}
  \quad \left[\text{cm}^2\text{/device or cm}^2\text{/bit}\right],
  \label{eq:cross_section}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the fluence in particles/cm$^2$.  The cross-section
is measured as a function of LET or proton energy and typically
exhibits a threshold below which no events are observed and a
plateau at high LET where every ion strike within the sensitive
volume causes an event.  The threshold LET (LET$_{\mathrm{th}}$) and
the saturation cross-section ($\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}}$) together
characterize the inherent hardness of the device~\cite{petersen1992rate}.

The on-orbit SEE rate is computed by convolving the measured
cross-section curve with the environment's differential LET
spectrum, a calculation codified in tools such as CREME96 and
OMERE~\cite{petersen2013revolution}.  For a device with
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}} = 10^{-6}$~cm$^2$/bit and
LET$_{\mathrm{th}} = 3$~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg in a LEO orbit, the
predicted upset rate is typically $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$~upsets/bit/day,
meaning a 16-Mbit configuration memory will experience
roughly 10--1,000~upsets per day without mitigation.

\subsection{Single-Event Upset (SEU)}

A single-event upset (SEU) is a non-destructive, recoverable change
in the logic state of a bistable storage element, a flip-flop,
SRAM cell, or register, caused by charge collection from a single
particle strike.  The struck node's voltage is transiently driven
past the switching threshold of the latch, causing it to adopt the
opposite state~\cite{dodd2003basic}.  In an SRAM-based FPGA, the
configuration memory that encodes the user's design is implemented
as an array of SRAM cells; an SEU in this array changes the
programmed function of a logic element, routing multiplexer, or
interconnect switch, potentially altering the circuit's behavior
in ways that are not immediately apparent without continuous
monitoring~\cite{quinn2007review}.

The critical charge for SEU in a modern 28-nm SRAM cell is on the
order of 1--10~fC, achievable by the recoil nucleus from a proton
nuclear interaction or by a moderate-LET heavy ion~\cite{baumann2005radiation}.
As process nodes have scaled, $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ has decreased roughly
proportionally with feature size, partially offset by reductions in
node capacitance and improvements in cell drive strength.

\subsection{Multiple Cell Upset (MCU)}

A multiple cell upset (MCU) occurs when a single particle deposits
sufficient charge to simultaneously flip two or more adjacent
memory cells.  MCUs are of particular concern because they can
defeat single-bit error correction codes (SECDED) that are
effective against isolated SEUs.  The probability of MCU scales
with LET and is strongly geometry-dependent: cells laid out in
adjacent rows and columns of the SRAM array are co-vulnerable,
while cells interleaved across different physical locations are
not~\cite{black2013mcu}.  In modern planar CMOS processes, MCU
events can involve tens of cells from a single high-LET heavy-ion
strike, while proton-induced MCUs typically involve two to four
cells from the recoil of a proton nuclear
reaction~\cite{baumann2005radiation}.

\subsection{Single-Event Transient (SET)}

A single-event transient (SET) is a voltage glitch on a
combinational logic node caused by charge collection at a
circuit junction.  Unlike an SEU, which corrupts a storage
element, an SET propagates through combinational logic and
may or may not be captured by a downstream flip-flop, depending
on the timing relationship between the transient and the
clock edge.  Fig.~\ref{fig:SET_diagram} illustrates the
generation of an SET at an inverter output, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:SET_FF} shows the conditions under which an SET
propagating through a logic cone is captured by a flip-flop.

In high-speed digital logic, SET pulse widths of tens to hundreds

SEUs a dominant error mechanism in modern FPGAs operating above
100~MHz~\cite{ferletcavrois2013set}.  At the lower clock rates
typical of nuclear I\&C applications, SET capture probability
is reduced, but long transients from high-LET events remain
a concern.

\subsection{Single-Event Functional Interrupt (SEFI)}

A single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) is an SEU in a control
register, finite state machine, or configuration memory that causes
a device to enter an erroneous operating mode, resetting itself,
locking up, or outputting invalid data, that requires a system-level
reset to clear.  In FPGAs, SEFIs in the configuration controller
or global control registers can cause a partial or complete loss
of the user's programmed logic, a far more serious consequence
than a single flipped bit in the data path~\cite{quinn2007review}.
SEFI rates are typically much lower than SEU rates per bit
(because control registers represent a small fraction of total
bits), but their operational consequence is disproportionately
severe.

\subsection{Single-Event Latchup (SEL)}

Single-event latchup (SEL) is a potentially destructive condition
in which a particle strike triggers the parasitic p-n-p-n thyristor
inherent in bulk CMOS processes, causing a low-impedance path to
form between the supply rails.  If not cleared by power cycling
within milliseconds, the resulting current can permanently destroy
the device through thermal runaway~\cite{sexton2003destructive}.
SEL susceptibility is strongly suppressed in silicon-on-insulator
(SOI) processes (which lack the bulk substrate needed for the
parasitic thyristor) and by epitaxial substrates with heavily
doped buried layers.  Modern commercial SRAM FPGAs fabricated
in bulk CMOS are SEL-susceptible and require current-limiting
power supply protection and power-cycling circuitry in space and
nuclear applications.

\subsection{Single-Event Burnout (SEB) and Gate Rupture (SEGR)}

Single-event burnout (SEB) and single-event gate rupture (SEGR)
are catastrophic destructive effects confined primarily to power
MOSFETs and bipolar transistors operating at high drain-source
voltages.  SEB occurs when a heavy-ion strike in the epitaxial
drain region of an OFF-state power MOSFET triggers a parasitic
n-p-n transistor, causing sustained high-current conduction that
thermally destroys the device~\cite{sexton2003destructive}.  SEGR
results from the momentary field enhancement in the gate oxide
caused by the ion-track plasma, which can exceed the oxide
breakdown field and permanently rupture the gate dielectric.
Figs.~\ref{fig:SEB_xsec}, \ref{fig:SEGR_diagram}, and
\ref{fig:SEGR_onset} illustrate the cross-sectional geometry and

any radiation-hardened design.

\section{FPGA Architecture and Radiation Vulnerability}
\label{sec:FPGA_arch}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{SRAM-Based FPGA Architecture}

Modern high-density FPGAs are organized as a two-dimensional array
of configurable logic blocks (CLBs), interconnected by a programmable
routing fabric and surrounded by dedicated I/O, clock management,
block RAM, DSP, and hard-IP peripherals.  The entire programmable
function of the device is encoded in a configuration memory bitstream
stored in an on-chip SRAM array, which for large devices may contain
50--500~million configuration bits~\cite{kuon2008fpga}.  The
configuration SRAM is loaded from external non-volatile storage
(typically QSPI flash or EEPROM) on power-up and retained in SRAM
for the duration of operation.

This architecture has a fundamental radiation vulnerability: every
bit of the configuration SRAM is continuously susceptible to SEU
throughout device operation.  An SEU in the configuration memory
can alter the function of a lookup table (LUT), change the routing
of a signal, enable or disable a flip-flop reset, or modify the
address decode of a block RAM, depending on which configuration
bit is struck.  Unlike an SEU in a user-data register, which affects
only the current computation, a configuration memory SEU silently
corrupts the device's programmed behavior until it is detected and
corrected~\cite{kastensmidt2014survey}.

The sensitivity of different FPGA configuration bit types to
functional corruption varies substantially.  Bits that control
LUT contents are always functionally significant; bits that control
unused routing resources may have no effect.  Studies of Xilinx
Virtex and Kintex FPGAs have found that roughly 15--30\% of
configuration bits are ``essential'', their upset causes a
detectable output error, with the remainder corresponding to
unused or redundant resources~\cite{quinn2007review,lee2014kintex}.
This essential bit fraction is critical for predicting the
functional upset rate from a measured raw upset rate.

\subsection{Radiation Sensitivity Across FPGA Generations}

Quinn et al.\ surveyed the radiation reliability concerns of Xilinx
FPGAs from the Virtex through Virtex-5 generations, finding that
each new process node brought reduced $Q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (worsening
raw SEU sensitivity per bit) but also reduced bit count per logic
function (improving functional upset rate
per implemented operation)~\cite{quinn2007review}.  SRAM cell
cross-sections measured at particle accelerators for 90-nm-class
devices (Virtex-4/5) are typically in the range of
$10^{-14}$--$10^{-13}$~cm$^2$/bit at high LET, corresponding
to functional upset rates of $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ upsets per
device per day in a representative LEO orbit.  Rakhman et al.\ 
characterized the SRAM configuration cells of a Xilinx Kintex-7
FPGA under both heavy-ion and proton irradiation, finding a
saturation cross-section of approximately $5 \times 10^{-14}$~cm$^2$/bit
and a proton cross-section of $\sim 10^{-15}$~cm$^2$/bit at
200~MeV~\cite{rakhman2017sram}.

For nuclear plant environments, the neutron-induced upset rate at
control-room locations (fast-neutron flux $< 10^3$~n/cm$^2$/s)
is orders of magnitude lower than in space and may be further
reduced by modest concrete shielding.  However, design-basis
accident scenarios that expose I\&C systems to elevated fluences
over hours to days require that the system either be immune to
configuration upsets or be capable of detecting and correcting
them within the plant's safety response time.

\section{Fault-Tolerant Mitigation Techniques}
\label{sec:mitigation}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Triple Modular Redundancy}

The foundational technique for SEU mitigation in digital systems
is triple modular redundancy (TMR), originally proposed by Lyons
and Vanderkulk in 1962 for improving the reliability of vacuum-tube
computers~\cite{lyons1962tmr}.  In TMR, the protected function is
replicated three times, and a majority voter selects the output of
the two (or three) agreeing copies.  A single SEU in any one replica
is masked because the two healthy copies outvote the corrupted one,
and the voter output remains correct.  The technique requires the
voter itself to be robust, either because it is implemented in
radiation-hard technology or because the voter is a simple gate
whose critical charge greatly exceeds that of the storage elements
it protects.

           replicas~\cite{lyons1962tmr,johnson2010voter}.}
  \label{fig:TMR_voter}

In FPGA implementations, TMR is applied at multiple granularities.
\emph{Full TMR} triplicates every logic element, flip-flop, and
routing resource in the design, providing maximum fault coverage
at the cost of roughly $3\times$ resource usage and power.
\emph{Partial TMR} applies triplication only to the most
safety-critical paths, reducing overhead while protecting against
the most consequential upsets~\cite{pratt2008partial}.
\emph{Register-file TMR} protects only the storage elements
(flip-flops and block RAMs) while leaving combinational logic
unprotected, trading reduced area for acceptance of transient
SET-induced errors that are not captured~\cite{sterpone2005tmr}.
Fig.~\ref{fig:fault_tolerant} illustrates these trade-offs, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:TMR_voter} shows the voter logic in detail.

The TMR approach is well-established in space applications and
has been demonstrated to reduce functional upset rates by
approximately two orders of magnitude in Xilinx Virtex-class
FPGAs at representative GCR fluences~\cite{wirthlin2015high}.
For nuclear I\&C applications, Sciber et al.\ demonstrated a
TMR-protected FPGA-based safety system for a research reactor
that maintained correct output through simulated configuration
upsets at a rate 500$\times$ the expected plant environment
level~\cite{SciDirect_TMR_Nuclear}.

\subsection{Configuration Scrubbing}

TMR corrects the voter output but does not repair the configuration
memory of the corrupted replica.  After a sufficient number of
upsets accumulate, two replicas may be simultaneously corrupted,
defeating majority voting.  Configuration scrubbing addresses
this by periodically refreshing the FPGA's configuration memory
from a known-good reference stored in non-volatile
memory~\cite{berg2008scrubbing}.

In \emph{external scrubbing}, an external processor reads the
current configuration bitstream from the FPGA, compares it
bit-by-bit with the golden reference, and writes back corrected
frames where differences are detected.  This approach can detect
and correct errors at rates determined by the scrub period,
which must be short compared to the mean time between double
upsets in the TMR replicas~\cite{heiner2009scrubbing}.  For a
device with $10^7$ configuration bits, a bit-upset rate of
$10^{-3}$~upsets/bit/day, and a required double-upset probability
below $10^{-6}$ per mission, the required scrub period is
on the order of seconds, achievable with standard scrubbing
controllers operating at JTAG bitstream rates~\cite{wirthlin2016scrubbing}.

\emph{Partial reconfiguration scrubbing} uses the FPGA vendor's
partial reconfiguration infrastructure to overwrite individual
configuration frames without resetting the entire device,
allowing scrubbing to proceed concurrently with normal device
operation~\cite{pratt2008partial}.  Xilinx Virtex/UltraScale
and Intel Stratix devices both support partial reconfiguration,
and open-source scrubbing controllers (e.g., the Scrub Engine
developed at Brigham Young University) implement this
approach~\cite{wirthlin2016scrubbing}.  Herrera-Alzu and
L\'{o}pez-Vallejo systematically characterized the scrubber
performance for Xilinx Virtex-5 FPGAs, demonstrating that
scrub periods below 50~ms reduced the effective functional
upset rate below $10^{-6}$ upsets/day at LEO
conditions~\cite{herreraalzu2013scrubbers}.

\subsection{Combined TMR-Scrubbing and Hybrid Approaches}

For the highest reliability requirements, TMR and scrubbing are
deployed together: TMR provides instant correction of single
upsets, while scrubbing prevents the accumulation of uncorrected
errors that would eventually defeat TMR~\cite{kastensmidt2014survey}.
The combination reduces the effective uncorrectable upset rate to
the probability of two upsets occurring within a single scrub
period before the first is corrected, typically several orders
of magnitude lower than either technique alone.

Hybrid approaches extend these concepts to the system level.
Stoddard et al.\ demonstrated a configuration in which an FPGA
running a spacecraft attitude control algorithm was protected by
TMR at the register level, partial-reconfiguration scrubbing at
the configuration level, and watchdog-timer reset at the system
level, achieving an estimated mean time between failures of
$> 10^5$~hours in a GEO radiation environment~\cite{stoddard2017hybrid}.
The architecture developed in Chapters~\ref{ch:digital_electronics} and~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark} for
nuclear plant monitoring adopts a similar layered approach, with
TMR protecting the safety logic, periodic configuration scrubbing
maintaining the configuration integrity, and a redundant
processor monitoring the voter-disagreement signal to detect any
uncorrected upset before it propagates to a safety output.

\subsection{Error Detection and Correction in Block RAMs}

Block RAMs (BRAMs) in FPGAs store application data, not
configuration bits, and are equally susceptible to SEUs.
Xilinx and Intel FPGAs provide optional built-in single-error
correction, double-error detection (SECDED) Hamming codes for
BRAM data, which correct single-bit upsets transparently and
flag double-bit upsets as uncorrectable~\cite{morgan2007comparison}.
For nuclear applications where the BRAM stores measurement data
or plant state variables, SECDED with periodic scrubbing provides
an acceptable level of protection, while dual-port BRAM with
cross-checking between two separate memory arrays provides an
additional layer of protection against MCU events that span
multiple bits within the same Hamming word.

\section{COTS FPGAs in Nuclear and Radiation-Critical Applications}
\label{sec:COTS_nuclear}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Cost-Performance Trade-off}

Purpose-built radiation-hardened FPGAs, such as the Microchip
RTG4 (130-nm flash-based, inherently SEU-immune configuration)
and the AMD/Xilinx XQRKU060 (16-nm FinFET, rad-hard by design),
provide guaranteed TID tolerance exceeding 300~krad(Si) and
quantified SEL immunity, but at unit prices of \$50,000--\$500,000
and with logic densities one to two generations behind commercial
equivalents~\cite{Microchip_RTG4,AMD_XQRKU060}.  For nuclear
plant I\&C modernization programs that may require hundreds of
processing nodes, this cost premium is prohibitive.

Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) FPGAs from the Xilinx 7-series,
UltraScale, and Intel Cyclone/Arria families offer $50$--$100\times$
lower cost per gate, higher logic density, and access to modern
soft-IP ecosystems, at the cost of no radiation-hardness guarantee
from the manufacturer~\cite{BISResearch_RadHardFPGA}.  The
engineering question is therefore not whether COTS FPGAs are
radiation-tolerant, but whether their radiation response, combined
with appropriate mitigation, can be characterized to a level of
assurance sufficient for the safety classification of the
application.

\subsection{Demonstrated COTS FPGA Performance in Radiation Environments}

Several accelerator and nuclear programs have demonstrated that
COTS FPGAs with TMR and scrubbing can meet stringent reliability
requirements at radiation levels consistent with nuclear plant
I\&C.  Lee et al.\ irradiated a Xilinx Kintex-7 FPGA (28-nm)
with protons at TRIUMF and with neutrons at Los Alamos National
Laboratory's Weapons Neutron Research facility, measuring a total
accumulated dose of 300~krad(Si) with no functional failure,
and recording zero configuration upsets attributable to TID
at that dose level~\cite{lee2014kintex}.  Wirthlin et al.\
demonstrated a Leon3 soft-core processor implemented on a
Kintex-7 FPGA that maintained correct program execution through
$3 \times 10^{10}$~n/cm$^2$ of 14-MeV neutron irradiation
with TMR+scrubbing active, corresponding to a ground-level
exposure equivalent of several decades of control-room
operation~\cite{wirthlin2016leon3}.

The CERN high-energy physics community has deployed COTS FPGAs
extensively in the LHC detector electronics, where annual doses
in some locations reach tens of krad and the SEE environment
from secondary shower particles is comparable to LEO.
Systematic characterization programs at CERN show that 28-nm
Kintex-7 and UltraScale devices exhibit no destructive effects
(SEL, SEB, SEGR) at bias voltages within datasheet ratings at
LET up to 62~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg, and that TMR+scrubbing
reduces functional upset rates below the $10^{-9}$/hour target
imposed by LHC safety systems~\cite{CERN_FPGA_Rad}.

Pignol reviewed the use of COTS components in safety-critical
space avionics and concluded that the combination of
architectural mitigation (TMR, watchdog), physical mitigation
(shielding, latchup current limiters), and comprehensive testing
is sufficient to achieve failure-in-time rates competitive with
purpose-built rad-hard parts, at substantially lower
cost~\cite{pignol2010cots}.  The same conclusion drives the
DOE GEMINA program, which targets AI/ML-enabled COTS digital
systems for advanced reactor I\&C~\cite{DOE_GEMINA}, and the
ANS-recommended roadmap for I\&C modernization at the existing
LWR fleet~\cite{ANS_ICModernization_2025}.

\subsection{Regulatory Pathway for COTS FPGAs in Nuclear Plants}

The NRC's regulatory framework for digital I\&C in nuclear plants
(10~CFR~50 Appendix~A, GDC~21--25; Regulatory Guide~1.152;
NUREG/CR-6263) does not prohibit COTS devices but requires that
the licensee demonstrate that the device meets the qualification
requirements for its safety classification, including radiation
qualification per IEEE~Std~7-4.3.2 and post-accident monitoring
per RG~1.97~\cite{nrc1997digital}.  The key qualification
documentation for an FPGA-based safety function includes: (1)
a radiation characterization data package demonstrating TID
tolerance, SEL immunity, and SEU cross-section measurement; (2)
a functional upset rate prediction for the plant-specific
radiation environment; (3) a mitigation architecture description
showing that the functional upset rate with TMR+scrubbing meets
the specified single failure criterion; and (4) a test program
demonstrating correct operation under the combined effects of
irradiation and functional loading~\cite{iaea2016design,natacad2018testing}.

\section{Testing Methodologies for FPGA Radiation Qualification}
\label{sec:testing}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

The qualification of FPGAs for radiation environments requires
experimental measurement of both TID and SEE parameters, since
analytical prediction from process parameters alone is not
sufficiently accurate for safety-relevant applications.
Fig.~\ref{fig:testing_approaches} illustrates the principal
testing approaches and their relationship to the environment
being simulated.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/2_6-testing-approaches.pdf}
  \caption{Summary of testing approaches for radiation hardness
           assurance of digital electronics, organized by
           radiation source, simulated environment, and
           measured effect~\cite{schwank2013radiation,coronetti2021systemlevel}.}
  \label{fig:testing_approaches}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Heavy-Ion Testing}

Heavy-ion irradiation at particle accelerators is the primary
technique for measuring SEE cross-sections as a function of LET
and for identifying the SEL, SEB, and SEGR thresholds of a device.
Facilities commonly used for FPGA radiation testing include the
Brookhaven National Laboratory Tandem Van de Graaff (LET range
1--100~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg), the Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory 88-Inch Cyclotron (LET up to 85~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg),
the Texas A\&M K500 Cyclotron, and the GANIL facility in
Caen, France~\cite{natacad2018testing}.

During a heavy-ion test, the device under test (DUT) is mounted
in a vacuum chamber, biased and clocked at normal operating
conditions, and exposed to a uniform beam of a selected heavy
ion species at controlled LET and fluence.  For FPGA testing,
two complementary readout strategies are used: \emph{static
testing}, in which the device is irradiated with configuration
memory pre-loaded and then readback to count configuration bit
flips, and \emph{dynamic testing}, in which the FPGA executes
a test design that monitors its own outputs for errors in
real time~\cite{quinn2007review}.  Dynamic testing captures
both configuration upsets and user-register upsets but is limited
in the depth of design validation per test run; static testing
provides complete configuration memory coverage but misses
upsets in the user logic flip-flops.

\subsection{Proton Testing}

Proton irradiation at energies of 50--200~MeV provides a
practical and cost-effective alternative to heavy-ion testing
for devices whose SEE cross-section vs. LET curve is required
through the proton-relevant range, and for devices intended for
use in proton-dominated environments (trapped proton belts,
low-altitude orbits).  Protons cause SEEs primarily through
nuclear elastic scattering (which produces silicon or oxygen
recoil nuclei with typical LET of 5--15~MeV$\cdot$cm$^2$/mg)
and through non-elastic nuclear reactions (which produce heavy
recoil fragments with higher LET)~\cite{petersen1992rate}.
The resulting SEU cross-section is lower than the heavy-ion
saturation cross-section by one to two orders of magnitude,
but the high proton fluences available at accelerators like
TRIUMF (Vancouver), IUCF (Bloomington), and the Paul Scherrer
Institute (Villigen) allow statistically meaningful
measurements~\cite{schwank2013radiation}.

Proton testing also accumulates significant TID during the SEE
test (at 100~MeV, 10$^{11}$~p/cm$^2$ corresponds to
$\sim$1~krad(Si)), which must be accounted for in test planning
to avoid confusing TID degradation with SEE events.

\subsection{Neutron Testing}

Neutron testing is the most directly relevant modality for
nuclear plant applications and for ground-level
qualification~\cite{stirk2023comparison}.  The upset mechanisms
for neutrons above $\sim$10~MeV are similar to protons,
nuclear elastic scattering producing silicon recoil nuclei
plus non-elastic spallation reactions producing heavier
fragments, but the neutron's lack of charge means it produces
no ionization along its own track and the DUT need not be
operated in vacuum.  This simplifies the experimental
setup considerably.

Several neutron irradiation facilities are available for
FPGA testing.  The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE)
ICE House provides a quasi-monoenergetic neutron spectrum
peaked at 800~MeV that is often used as a reference for
atmospheric cosmic-ray testing~\cite{wirthlin2016leon3}.  The
Indiana University Cyclotron Facility (IUCF) produced
monoenergetic 200-MeV neutrons, now primarily replaced by
the Triangle Universities Nuclear Laboratory (TUNL) and the
nTOF facility at CERN for intermediate energies.  For reactor
neutron environments, fission reactor irradiation facilities
such as the MIT reactor's fission converter
beam~\cite{harling2006fission} and the
Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR) provide
thermal-to-fast neutron spectra representative of ex-core
I\&C locations.

The standard approach for neutron SEE testing of FPGAs follows
the JEDEC JESD89A protocol: the device is irradiated with a
known neutron fluence at the test facility's reference spectrum,
and the measured upset count is scaled to the terrestrial
sea-level equivalent fluence using the spectral overlap
integral~\cite{jedec2006jesd89a}.  Stirk et al.\
compared three neutron testing approaches (monoenergetic,
white-spectrum, and reactor) for a complex system-on-chip
and found that the accelerator white-spectrum test at LANSCE
gave the closest agreement with reactor exposure results,
while monoenergetic beam tests required more careful spectral
correction~\cite{stirk2023comparison}.

\subsection{Total Ionizing Dose Testing}

TID testing is performed by exposing the device to a
controlled dose from a $^{60}$Co gamma source or an X-ray
irradiator at a specified dose rate, with the device biased
in a representative operating condition.  The JEDEC/MIL-STD-883
Method 1019 protocol specifies the irradiation conditions,
readout intervals, and post-irradiation anneal procedure
required for military and space qualification~\cite{schwank2013radiation}.

A critical subtlety in TID testing is dose-rate dependence:
many MOS devices exhibit enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity
(ELDRS), in which the damage at low dose rates ($<$0.1~rad(Si)/s)
exceeds that at high dose rates (1--100~rad(Si)/s) for the
same total dose~\cite{fleetwood2013total}.  ELDRS occurs in
bipolar transistors and certain MOS linear circuits but is
less pronounced in digital CMOS; it is nonetheless a
qualification concern for reactor I\&C systems that accumulate
dose over decades at rates of millirad per hour rather than
the rad-per-second rates used in accelerated laboratory tests.
The MIL-STD-883 Method 1019 elevated-temperature anneal
procedure is designed to provide a conservative bound on
ELDRS without requiring multi-year low-dose-rate
exposures~\cite{shaneyfelt2008qualification}.

\subsection{Ground-Level and Accelerated System Testing}

For applications where the primary concern is the
terrestrial cosmic-ray neutron environment, system-level
testing at the actual deployment site or at a representative
high-altitude location provides the most directly applicable
qualification data~\cite{coronetti2021systemlevel}.  However,
at sea-level neutron flux ($\sim 10^{-2}$~n/cm$^2$/s above
10~MeV), accumulating $10^{10}$~n/cm$^2$, a fluence corresponding
to several decades of ground-level exposure, would require
$\sim 3 \times 10^{10}$~seconds, making direct ground-level
testing impractical for qualification purposes.  Accelerated
testing at mountain-top observatories (e.g., Mauna Kea,
5~km altitude, flux $\sim 10\times$ sea level) or accelerator
facilities bridging this gap.

Adell et al.\ and Artola et al.\ developed simulation frameworks
using GEANT4 to predict ground-level neutron upset rates from
device-level cross-section measurements, validating the approach
against measured field failure data from commercial
servers~\cite{adell2008assessing,artola2021benchmarks}.  The
GEANT4 Monte Carlo toolkit~\cite{agostinelli2003geant4}, which
models hadronic and electromagnetic interactions from eV to TeV
energies, has become the standard simulation infrastructure for
predicting SEE rates in complex radiation environments including
both atmospheric and reactor neutron spectra.

\section{FPGA Verification Challenges in Nuclear Beam Environments}
\label{sec:verification_challenges}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

Beyond the physics of radiation damage, the experimental
characterization of FPGA upset susceptibility at nuclear beam
facilities presents several practical constraints that inform the
design of in-beam test instruments such as those developed in
Chapters~\ref{ch:digital_electronics} and~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark}.

\textbf{State-volume coverage.}  An SEE cross-section measurement
requires observing a statistically significant number of events.  The
cross-section for a single memory cell is of order $10^{-14}$ to
$10^{-12}$~cm$^2$, so at a flux of $10^5$--$10^7$~n/cm$^2$/s a single
cell would require impractically long exposures to accumulate even one
event on average.  Maximising the monitored state population is
therefore essential.

\textbf{Observability without intrusion.}  Reading every flip-flop
off-chip individually during irradiation is infeasible for large state
populations and introduces additional wiring susceptible to radiation
and noise.  A compact on-chip checker that compresses the health of
the state array into a small digital flag is preferable.

\textbf{Configuration vs.\ data upsets.}  Any experimental design must
be capable of distinguishing a user-data upset from a configuration
memory upset.  The two mechanisms require different mitigation
strategies: data-path redundancy (DMR/TMR) addresses the former, while
periodic configuration scrubbing addresses the latter.

\textbf{Telemetry reliability.}  The UART or other communication
channel used to export results is itself implemented in FPGA resources
and therefore subject to the same configuration upsets.  A robust
transmitter design reduces the risk that the observability channel
itself is corrupted during an experiment.

These constraints motivate the specific design choices made in the
FFSR shift-register test structure (Chapter~\ref{ch:digital_electronics})
and the fixed-point thermal control benchmark
(Chapter~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark}), both of which employ on-chip
redundancy comparison, compact telemetry encoding, and large monitored
state populations to maximize the probability of detecting
radiation-induced errors within the available beam time.

\section{Summary}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter has established the physical and engineering framework
for the FPGA-based nuclear instrumentation systems developed in
Chapters~\ref{ch:digital_electronics} and~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark}.  The three radiation environments
of concern, space, nuclear plant, and terrestrial cosmic-ray,
each impose distinct TID and SEE demands on digital electronics.
TID damage in MOS gate oxides accumulates throughout device
operation and degrades transistor performance at doses that,
for control-room I\&C in nuclear plants, remain well within the
tolerance of modern CMOS processes.  The more consequential
threat is stochastic single-event effects, which occur at
arbitrarily low dose rates: SEUs corrupt FPGA configuration
memory silently and persistently, SEFIs can interrupt device
operation, and SELs can destroy devices if power cycling is not
promptly applied.

Triple modular redundancy, applied at the register and routing
level within the FPGA fabric, corrects single upsets
instantaneously without interrupting the safety function;
periodic configuration scrubbing prevents upset accumulation
that would eventually defeat TMR.  The combination of these
two techniques reduces the uncorrectable upset probability to
levels that can be demonstrated to meet the single-failure
criterion of nuclear safety standards.  COTS FPGAs from the
28-nm and 16-nm Kintex-7 and UltraScale families have been
qualified for radiation environments substantially more
demanding than nuclear plant I\&C through heavy-ion, proton,
and neutron testing at national accelerator facilities, and
the regulatory pathway for their use in safety-related
applications is established in IAEA SSG-39 and NRC
Regulatory Guide 1.152.  The dissertation now turns to the
perovskite materials and device investigations
(Chapters~\ref{ch:CsFAPbI}--\ref{ch:perovskite_detectors}) and
thermal sensing concepts (Chapter~\ref{ch:thermal_sensing}) before
returning to experimentally validate these FPGA principles:
Chapter~\ref{ch:digital_electronics} presents the Flip-Flop Shift
Register (FFSR) verification structure tested under fast neutron
irradiation, and Chapter~\ref{chap:fpga_benchmark} extends that
campaign with a computationally richer fixed-point thermal control
benchmark.
